Abstract—Vehicle routing is one of the oldest, yet critical,
problems of the logistics sector. Globalization and the rise of
e-commerce as well as technological advances have allowed the
development of a profitable routing software market, which has
recently attracted the attention of technology giants. Among the
plethora of routing software alternatives, Google’s open source
optimization suite, OR-Tools, looks particularly promising, pro-
viding a useful and affordable optimization tool for small and
medium sized logistic companies and applications. The goal
of this paper is to scientifically evaluate the accuracy, and
performance of OR-Tools suite by examining the quality of its
solution results in a set of capacitated vehicle routing problems
with delivery time windows, also known as capacitated vehicle
routing problem with time-windows (CVRPTW) or VRPTW. A
comparative analysis is conducted by solving Solomon’s VRPTW
problems for a small (25 customers), medium (50 customers), and
large (100 customers) number of customers with OR-Tools and
comparing the results with the known optimal solutions for a
fixed maximum solution time and varied number of available
vehicles. Additionally, in this paper we demonstrate a real case
of application of the OR-Tool tools to a real problem concerning
the online distribution of dairy products. But given the very
nebulous definition of the online character of a problem, OR-
Tool does not include online VRP algorithms. For this reason,
we will convert the solution of the classic stationary VRP using
OR-Tools into a solution for Online VRP.
Index Terms—VRP, OR Tools, CVRPTW, VRPTW
I. INTRODUCTION
The rapid development of technology and evolution of busi-
ness models in addition to ever-increasing globalization and
the rise of e-commerce have put transportation networks and
supply chains under an unprecedented strain. This complicated
environment and current environmental concerns make the
optimization of the supply chain vital to more and more
companies in order to remain competitive, profitable and up-to
date with new, more stringent regulations [1].
Although many technical solutions have been introduced the
truth is that most of small and medium sized businesses still
rely on instinct, experience, or simplistic methods as the sole
basis for their routing practices. A cause of this phenomenon
is that the purchase and use of sophisticated routing or
optimization software is often an expensive investment. Using
open source optimization software is also challenging because
it usually has severe limitations, or it is too complicated
to implement in a real-world application. Recently, however,
there has been a growing interest in the development of
publicly available technology tools, including optimization and
routing tools, by big technological giants. An interesting case
is the open source optimization suite OR-Tools which is free,
simple to use, constantly updated and backed by one of the
world’s biggest IT companies, Google [2].
The cost differentials between static Vehicle Routing Prob-
lems (VRP) and their dynamic counterparts have been the
subject of numerous studies. Findings from the literature offer
some insight into the average cost increases, despite the fact
that the exact percentage increase in objective costs when
converting a static VRP to an online VRP can vary. According
to research, the conversion of a static VRP to an online VRP
results in an increase in operational expenses. For example,
the results suggest that the expenses associated with online
routing may be somewhat higher than those of static routing,
which is indicative of inefficiencies that result from real-
time adaptation to changing conditions [3]. In addition the
competitive ratio provides an additional viewpoint on cost
disparities by contrasting the performance of online algorithms
with that of the optimal static solution. This ratio can reveal
cost increases ranging from 20% to 40% when transitioning
to online systems, depending on situational parameters such
as route density and request frequency, according to certain
studies [3]. Finally, empirical analyses have revealed scenarios
in which online VRPs may incur higher costs as a result of
the increased travel distances and delays necessary to accom-
modate dynamic requests. In environments with significant
variability in customer requests, online solutions can result in
operational cost increases, as some literature cites a range of
15% to 35% [4]. Nevertheless, it is important to acknowledge
that dynamic policies can be more responsive in specific
situations, despite the fact that they may result in higher costs
in practice as a result of routing fluctuations.

The objective was to study the real-time optimization of a ver-

In this online model of the vehicle routing problem, new dairy

no additional requests emerge after its beginning.
II. BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK
A. The Vehicle Routing Problem
The basic VRP calls for the determination of the optimal set
of routes to be performed by a fleet of vehicles to serve a given
set of customers from a central depot and is one of the most
significant and studied combinatorial optimization problems.
The VRP is classified as an NP-hard problem. In transportation
and logistics, the VRP presents a major challenges marked by
several variants that fit particular operational requirements and
constraints. The main groups of VRP variants consist in:
1)
Capacitated Vehicle Routing Problem (CVRP): This
well-established variant entails the identification of optimal
routes for a fleet of vehicles, ensuring that the total demand of
served customers does not exceed the capacity of any vehicle
[5], [6].
2) Vehicle Routing Problem with Time Windows (VRPTW):
In this variation, the routing process is further complicated
by the requirement for vehicles to service customers within
specific time frames [7].
3) Vehicle Routing Problem with Pick-up and Delivery
(VRPPD): This variation is concerned with situations in which
vehicles are responsible for the collection and delivery of
goods at predetermined locations. In addition to the necessity
of adhering to specific routing sequences for both pick-ups
and deliveries, constraints encompass vehicle capacities [8].
4) Heterogeneous Fleet Vehicle Routing Problem (HFVRP):
The HFVRP effectively models the realistic fleet compositions
found in many transport operations, in contrast to the homo-
geneous fleet models typically considered in traditional VRP
frameworks [9].
5) Multi-Depot Vehicle Routing Problem (MDVRP): This
variation of the VRP is applicable to situations in which
multiple depots contain vehicles that can distribute goods to
a variety of customer locations. This complexity introduces
additional logistical considerations, as it necessitates the effi-
cient management of routes that originate from multiple depots
simultaneously [10].
6) Open Vehicle Routing Problem (OVRP): A distinctive
method that eliminates the necessity for vehicles to return
to their initial location after delivering goods. This results in
distinct considerations in route optimization and is particu-
larly relevant in situations where the return journey is either
economically unfeasible or unnecessary [11].
7) Routing Issues for Electric Vehicles (E-VRPs): E-VRPs
are exclusively concerned with the adaptation of conventional
VRP frameworks to electric vehicles. They take into account
the effects of electric vehicle performance on route efficiency,
battery charging times, and driving range [12].
B. Existing solution methods for the VRP
The VRP includes a variety of solution methods that illus-
trate its complexity and multiple formulations. The methods
can be classified into three primary categories: exact algo-
rithms, approximate algorithms, and heuristic/metaheuristic
algorithms. Each category provides unique benefits and is ap-
propriate for varying problem scales and specifications. Exact
algorithms, by means of exhaustive search of the search space,
offer definite solutions for the VRP. These approaches use
mathematical programming to identify best paths and consist
in linear programming, branch-and-bound, and branch-and-
cut techniques. Nevertheless, scalability problems sometimes
limit their applicability to small-to medium-sized instances
of the problem by their computational intensity. Pisinger and
Røpke stress the need of exact techniques for different VRP
forms and their shortcomings in effectively handling more
significant cases [13]. While it is usually unworkable for
complicated VRPs involving many consumers or constraints,
this category is helpful when ensuring the best solution is
vital [14]. Approximate Algorithms, this group of methods
seeks to provide solutions that are sufficiently good but do not
guarantee optimality. They often employ a mix of problem-
specific heuristics and optimization techniques, designed to
quickly yield feasible solutions. A notable method in this cat-
egory includes the use of greedy algorithms, which construct a
solution by iteratively selecting the next best option based on a
heuristic criterion [14]. These algorithms are particularly use-
ful when time constraints prioritize rapid solution generation
over optimality, especially in dynamic logistical settings.
Heuristic and metaheuristic algorithms have attracted popu-
larity in the literature since they offer strong solutions able to
solve more general and more difficult cases of the VRP. While
metaheuristics (e.g., genetic algorithms, simulated annealing,
tabu search, and ant colony optimization) offer frameworks for
more fully exploring the solution space, heuristics operate by
producing solutions based on rule-of- thumb strategies without
extensive computational overhead. Especially metaheuristics
can dynamically change their approaches depending on the
performance of past iterations, so improving the quality of
the solutions over time [15], [16]. Empirical studies [17]
and empirical data [18] have shown, for example, notable
success of hybrid algorithms combining several metaheuristic
approaches. Their adaptability qualifies them for real-world
VRP uses since it combines efficiency with the capacity to
manage constraints and variability usually present in logistics
operations. As the difficulties in VRP solving have changed,
hybrid algorithms—which combine the strengths of exact,

III. OR-TOOLS SUITE FOR VRP PROBLEMS
A. OR-Tools
OR-Tools is an open source software suite for optimization,
tuned for solving several variants of VRP, flows, integer and
linear programming, and constraint programming. OR-Tools
API can be used in some of the world’s most popular program-
ming languages like C++, C#, Python and Java. OR-Tools suite
provides modeling tools for routing and optimization problems
and Google’s GLOP and CP-SAT mathematical. Third party
open-source (SCIP, GLPK) or professional (Gurobi, CPLEX)
solvers can also be used parallel to OR-Tools.
With respect to vehicle routing, OR tools are able to solve
most of the major VRP variants with no limits on the number
of vehicles or customers. The VRP variants covered by OR-
Tools are:
• VRP
• CVRP (there is the capability of solving the problem with
dropping visits)
• VRPTW (apart from time windows, loading and service
times may be considered)
• CVRPTW
• PDP (Pick-up and Delivery Problem, a VRP variation
which includes Pick-Ups)
The suite can handle both symmetrical and asymmetrical
problems and of homogeneous or not-homogeneous vehicle
fleets. It should be noted that the methods used to solve routing
problems are not exact, with OR-Tools instead relying on a
library of heuristic and metaheuristic algorithms.
Solomon’s problems [21] are a set of CVRPTW problems
with known optimal solutions, which is often used for bench-
marking solution algorithms. Problems are categorized into six
subsets according to several factors that affect the behavior of
routing and scheduling algorithms.
The name of the subsets consists of two parts. The first part
indicates the problem’s geographical data structure, with the
possible values being R (randomly generated), C (clustered)
or RC (randomly clustered), as shown in Fig. 1. The second
part indicates low or high maximum capacity vehicles taking
the values 1 and 2 accordingly.
Each problem instance consists of 1 depot point and 100
customer points (Euclidean graph), where travel times equal
the corresponding distances. For each such problem, smaller
problems have been created by considering only the first 25
or 50 customers.
Fig. 1. Different Solomon problem types.
The customer coordinates are identical for all problems
within one type (i.e., R, C and RC). The problems differ
with respect to the width of the time windows with some of
them having very tight time windows, while others have time
windows which are hardly constraining.
B. Methodology
In this research the entirety of Solomon’s problem set is
tested. Python code using OR-Tools Python interface inputs
the data for each individual problem, which includes coor-
dinates, demands, time windows, service times and vehicle
capacities. Cases are tested with three different numbers of
available vehicles equal to the number of vehicles in the known
optimal solution plus 50% and 100% additional vehicles
(rounded up). The maximum allowed computational time for
all problems is fixed to 60 seconds. The table below shows
the total number of optimization attempts for each type of

B. Problem definition for our real life test Case
A single transport vehicle is utilized, with sufficient capacity
to fulfill all demands. Initially, the model computes an optimal
route visiting all retail locations (a total of 30), starting from
the company’s facilities (DEPOT – node 0), based on the input
data (temporal sequence of demands and locations of retail
points). As the vehicle visits a number of locations, a new
demand for products arises, prompting the model to recalculate
an optimized route that incorporates the new demand alongside
the remaining unfulfilled ones. This process repeats as new
demands emerge until all retail locations are visited. The
demand study timeframe is divided into four time windows
(0-120, 120-240, 240-360, 360-480 minutes) – (08:00-10:00,
10:00-12:00, 12:00-14:00, 14:00-16:00) . Each new request is
considered for fulfillment from the subsequent time window
following its emergence. The waiting time for each node’s
demand fulfillment was set to be sufficiently large to ensure
that, in the event of multiple simultaneous requests within
the same time window due to randomness, the results remain
unaffected. The selection of both the number of submodels
within the method (with a maximum of 3) and the number
of retail points included in each submodel, as well as the
time windows in which respective demands are met is random,
following the steps below:
(a) A number between 1 and 30 is selected, indicating the
number of nodes to be served in the first submodel’s
resolution.
(b) A set of numbers between 1 and 30 is chosen, corre-
sponding to the number selected in step (a). These nodes
will be served in the first submodel’s resolution.
(c) For each node selected in step (b), a number between 1
and 4 is chosen, indicating the time window in which it
will be served.
(d) A number between 1 and [30 - (number from step (a)] is
selected, determining the number of nodes to be served
in the second submodel’s resolution.
(e) A set of numbers between 1 and 30, corresponding to the
number selected in step (d), is chosen. These nodes will
be served in the second submodel’s resolution, excluding
those already considered in the first submodel.
(f) For each node selected in step (e), a number between 2
and 4 is chosen, indicating the time window in which

In the first submodel, the initial node (DEPOT) is set as 0.
In the second, the initial node is selected as the last visited
node from the first submodel’s route within the first time
window. By solving these subproblems, the total travel time
of the routes is calculated, and by summing the individual arc
distances, the total distance traveled by the vehicle is also de-
termined, both for each submodel and the entire online model.
A total of 50 models were studied. Based on the adopted
methodology, the total transportation times of 50 dynamic
models of the vehicle routing problem were determined, along
with the corresponding times of each model’s submodels.
The automatic solution strategy was applied in combination
with the automatic improvement algorithm. Additionally, given
the distances between nodes, the traveled distance of each
submodel and the total model distance were calculated.

between the behavior of the online and the offline system. In

route execution. All demands are placed in chronological order
and can only be fulfilled from the subsequent time window
onward. The travel times of the static and dynamic versions
were compared (Fig. 5), along with their respective total
distances (Fig. 4). The numerical data, as well as the average

the sum of the individual submodels’ times.

three. This occurred because, during the selection process for
the submodel elements, in the second submodel of each case,
the examined nodes were all those not considered in the first.
V. CONCLUSIONS
The outcome of comparative analysis shows that OR-Tools
can be useful and accurate enough to meet the needs of
small and medium sized CVRPTW applications. Small (25
customers) and medium (50 customers) sized problems were
solved on average over 75% of the time. For large problems
(100 customers), cases with small capacity vehicles were
solved on average over 87.5% of the time, while cases with
high capacity vehicles were difficult to solve. The quality of
the solutions produced, for most cases, was found to be good
with average deviation less than 10% from the known optimal
solutions. Finally, it was observed that a significant increase
in the number of vehicles available, as inputs, was required
to obtain solutions, but most of the additional vehicles were
not used and the actual number of vehicles needed was less
than 30% of the number of vehicles in the optimal solution.
The results were obtained within strict time limits, which
suggests that OR-Tools could be used reliably in real-world
problems. Moreover, based on the above real world use case,
the static model, in which all necessary data are known before
the start of the route, seems to demonstrate better behavior and
performance.
