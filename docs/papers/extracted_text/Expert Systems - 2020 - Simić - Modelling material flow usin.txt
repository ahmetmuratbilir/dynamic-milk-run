Abstract
Material flow management refers to the analysis and specific optimization of the
inventory-production system. Material flow can be characterized as the organized
flow of material in a production process with the required sequence determined by a
technological procedure. The Milk run system assures the transportation of materials
at the right time and in an optimal manner. It should be combined with the Kanban
system to highlight when something is required in the production process. This paper
presents biological swarm intelligence, in general, and a particular model, particle
swarm optimization (PSO), for modelling material flow using a Milk run system
supported by a Kanban system in the automotive industry. The aim of this study is to
create a new model for the optimal number of trailers for one train and optimal num-
ber of containers in a tugger train system when the route time period has been
defined. A new modified PSO approach for integrating inventory-production in a
unique optimization model is used. The major modification to the original PSO is
using the capacity of a container instead of a velocity component. Each new Kanban
trigger is checked, and the total timing for the Milk run delivery solution is calculated
for the necessary raw material capacity for each shop floor.

INTRODUCTION
Material flow management refers to the analysis and specific optimization of material and energy flows that arise during the manufacturing of
products and provision of services. Material flow can be characterized as an organized flow of material in a production process with the required
sequence determined by a technological procedure. It is a summary of operations presented by material conveying, storage, packaging and
weighing, and technological manipulations and other works directly related to the production process. Planning and dimensioning material flow
challenges are difficult to overcome, especially in scenarios characterized by many hard constraints and by well-established processes
(Wagner, 2006).
Furthermore, we should differentiate between internal and cross-company material flow management; whereas internal material flow refers
to the movements of substances or materials within a company (Simic, 2012), cross-company (external) material flow describes the path of a
material along the value-added chain (Simic, 2015). Thus, successful material flow management links the structural analysis of material flow with
data available in business information systems with recent paradigms, such as enterprise resource planning, logistics information systems, big data,
artificial intelligence, in general, and soft-computing techniques, in particular (Ilin, 2017).

A Milk run system is one of the most efficient and popular models used to improve logistic operations in every serial production environment
such as the automotive industry, electrical industry, electronic industry, mechanical industry, food distribution, and military. It can effectively
reduce inventory, while increasing the material flow between warehouses and shop floors, and the usage rate of the vehicles, therefore decreas-
ing costs and improving productivity (Simic-1, 2019a, 2019b). The use of a Milk run system is based on tugger trains for industrial material trans-
portation, which have shown three important tendencies: (a) tugger train systems are being mainly adopted for material supply; (b) adoption of
tugger train systems has recently increased following a linear trend and (c) the use of tugger train systems in other sectors is consistently growing
(Urru, 2018).
However, a Milk run system has some limitations. It must be implemented with other material flow systems, such as a traditional Kanban sys-
tem, an E-kanban system, some sets of constant work-in-progress (CONWIP) mechanisms, or software for material needs control. Therefore, this
study focuses on systems that incorporate the existing Milk run production-delivery system presented in (Simic-2, 2019a, 2019b), which regulates
both the Kanban system and complete material flow in an inventory-production system. The Milk run system assures that inventory is transported
at the right time and in an optimal way. The objective of this paper is to present the inception of the pull system and the analyses of system per-
formance based on simulation study.
This paper presents biological swarm intelligence, in general, and a particular model, the modified particle swarm optimization (PSO), for
modelling material flow using a Milk run system supported by a Kanban system for use in the automotive industry. The aim of this research is to
create model to minimize inventory-production costs.
The rest of the paper is organized as follows: Section 2 provides an overview of material flow management and a review of related studies.
Section 3 describes the PSO implemented in material flow using the Milk run system and Kanban system. This section also describes the dataset
used in the implementation. Experimental results and a discussion are presented in Section 4, and finally, Section 5 gives the concluding remarks.

MATERIAL FLOW AND RELATED WORK
The term material flow covers a broad spectrum of methods and approaches in the literature. Most contemporary organizations are facing many
dilemmas, the most important being determination of ways of acquiring or maintaining their position in a competitive environment. Endeavours
to reduce inventory, in the context of operation management, are often related to lean manufacturing, which focuses on removing waste from
activities and processes that do not add value to products, thus creating an efficient inventory-production system (Chen, 2010).
The strategic aim of all production enterprises is to achieve the highest profit possible. Toward this end, attributes of lean production that are
related to and support the reduction of production costs are used. By using theoretical knowledge and a detailed analysis of a material flow,
including mapping of individual production processes, it is possible to obtain the necessary data sets that identify savings options by increasing
efficiency, including cost savings. The methods for modelling and simulation used for the optimization of material flow are presented in
(Kodym, 2019).
Competitive prevalence can be achieved by analysing and optimizing material flow, which contributes to a reduction of the company costs.
Material flow in a production organization shows how the production system is organized. In research paper (Krolczyk, 2015), the current state of
material flow in a production company in the automotive sector is analysed. Material flow optimization has been proposed, and it consists of the
creation of a program of internal transport and optimization of the location of the working stands on the shop floors. Furthermore, the current
problems of the company's internal transport system are highlighted and solutions to those problems are proposed.
Material handling of flow is one of the most crucial issues that should be considered to eliminate waste and reduce the cost and delivery of
the product based on the “just in time” method. An effective transportation management system optimized with the Milk run concept, must be
implemented to control the cost of transportation and inventory. The Milk run material supply system consists of cyclic trips, where goods are col-
lected from one supplier and delivered to several customers. The objective of this optimization is minimization of the total material handling and
inventory holding costs, which could boost sales or increase the profit margin of the organization (Patel, 2017).
A systematic approach which consists of Milk run route optimization with a mixed integer linear programming model that comprises a con-
tainer loading heuristic that increases the utilization of Milk run vehicles and a time interval assignment heuristic that prevents overlapping of Milk
run vehicles at the depots of a manufacturer has been proposed in (Aksoy, 2016).
A mixed-integer nonlinear programming (MINLP) model to determine the number of Kanban and the transported batch size in a multistage
supply chain system was developed in (Sarker, 1998). In this regard, the objective was to minimize inventory holding, transportation of each con-
tainer, and setup costs.
System metaheuristic methods are used to determine the optimal Kanban quantity and withdrawal lot sizes in a supply chain. A genetic algo-
rithm and a hybrid of a genetic algorithm and simulated annealing (SA) are used (Widyadana, 2010). To reduce the computational time required to
obtain an optimal solution, PSO and SA algorithms as metaheuristic methods are used to solve a large MINLP to determine the number of Kanban
in a cellular manufacturing system with consideration of the rework process (Aghajani, 2012).

A case study and simulation analysis on a Kanban system incorporating a Milk run operation used to add raw materials to the production pro-
cess is presented in (Chee, 2012). Here, the study data were collected for 10 weeks. The proposed Kanban model was simulated to test its various
performances including total output, average flow time, average work-in-process, and average waiting time. Response surface methodology was
adopted to generate suitable representative regression models. For all performance measures, the simulation results revealed that the proposed
system consistently outperforms the push system currently used in the company. The operators in the proposed Kanban system have a lower uti-
lization, even with the additional material handling task.
The problems of transportation planning have characteristics that match the specific skills of multi agent systems (MAS). These systems are
able to cope with inter-organizational and event-oriented planning. The study presented in (Pinheiro, 2019) evaluates different collaboration strat-
egies for dynamic vehicle routing and autonomous vehicle interaction as well as communication between vehicles in Milk run operations using
MAS, which allow vehicle interaction and autonomous responses in unplanned events. Auxiliary vehicles are used to meet the tasks that cannot
be met by regular vehicles with unnecessary additional cost.
In the logistics system, there are many assembly plants and numerous part suppliers located in close proximity to each other. The suppliers
collect the parts and transport them to the assembly plants in the small-batch and multi-frequency mode. A multi-factory Milk run pickup problem
with uncertain demand and frequency in China’s automobile industry is presented in (Wu, 2018). Considering the balance between inventory cost
and distribution cost, a mixed-integer programming model was built for this problem and converted into a robust optimization model by the
Chernoff–Hoeffding theorem. Then the adaptive genetic algorithm and local search were combined into a general hybrid heuristic algorithm to
successful solve the problem.
The fundamental inventory-production problem that needs to be solved is the problem of bringing each component to the line only at the
moment and at the quantity needed to feed the assembly line. The introduction of decentralized storage areas, called supermarkets (SM) made it
possible for frequent deliveries of small batches of components to be made (Emde, 2017). Supermarkets are located between the production line
and the central warehouse. A discrete event simulation model is developed, and a mathematical model based on a PSO optimization is used to
support the simulation. The hybrid model is finally applied to practical situations and presented in (Kundua, 2019).
The practical implementation of a Milk run system by Toyota Group in automotive factories in Indonesia was analysed in (Hardi, 2019). The
Milk run system was designed to collect goods from two or more suppliers and send them to the customer. At the same time, the capacity of a
truck was optimized to increase the cargo for fully loaded distribution. The time and number of pickup schedules of the Milk run determine the
performance of the logistics system, while the route design and scheduling are the key factors that have an impact on the cost and also determine
the smoothness of the continuous pickup. The key goal of a Milk run operation is to minimize the inventory (holding) cost and transportation cost.
The advantage of a Milk run operation is cost reduction, but it also supports green logistics by decreasing carbon dioxide emissions (CO2) by
reducing the number of trucks used.
An optimization model implemented in a hybrid environment that contains constraint logic programming, mathematical programming, and
metaheuristics for the capacitated vehicle routing problem with pick-up and alternative delivery is presented in (Sitek, 2019). The zero-inventory
approach, which has been widely adopted by many automobile companies, seems to be impractical in certain real production contexts as it
requires an inventory of all parts but in low amounts. A mixed integer programming formulation for a new model that can simultaneously deter-
mine the trip routes for collecting automobile parts and minimize total production costs and inbound logistics is shown in (Mao, 2019).

MODELLING THE MILK RUN AND KANBAN SYSTEM IN MATERIAL FLOW
To obtain a perfect inventory-production system based on the Milk run system, most of the operations should be automated and controlled. Over
the years, many production control systems have been proposed within various factories, such as push-like buffered production lines, pull-like
Kanban systems, and hybrid-like CONWIP (Song, 2013). For buffered production lines the input quantities are the sizes of the buffers between
each of the machines, that is, the workplaces. For Kanban systems, the input quantities are the number of production and transfer Kanbans. For
CONWIP systems, there is only one input quantity, which is the number of CONWIP cards (Herer, 2000).

Milk run system
The basic planning process of a Milk run system is divided in four phases, as is shown in Figure 1. Every choice made in one phase impacts the
next. This impact is not easily assessable until all the planning has been completed. Therefore, the planning process is based on a loop system so
that, in every planning phase, decisions made in the previous phases can be called into question, and relevant parameters may be adjusted. This
planning approach requires a high level of expertise and competence to ensure that the right adjustments are made and a consistent and useful
result is obtained.

Kanban system
Kanban systems were initially developed in Japan to manage multistage manufacturing systems. Every manufacturing stage has a target local
inventory level for the local inventories, where the local inventory can be considered the inventory space at one shop floor. The customer demand
information is transmitted upward stage by stage.
More specifically, it states that, whenever an item is withdrawn from the local inventory, a card is issued to the corresponding production unit
to signal that a new part should be produced (Duri, 2000). This policy also operates on a pull mechanism. Its advantage is that the number of parts
at each stage is limited by the number of Kanbans at that stage. The disadvantages are the indirect flow of demand information from customers
to suppliers and the lack of utilization of the inventory information at downstream stages.
A CONWIP system can be viewed as a single stage Kanban system, in which a single type of card is used to control the total amount of inven-
tories permitted in the entire system. Essentially, only one target inventory level for the entire system is triggered when the most upstream pro-
duction unit needs to produce a new part (Takahashi, 2005).
Traditionally, a Kanban is a tag-like card (Kanban is Japanese meaning “a card” or “visible record”). One Kanban card must be attached to each
container in the output store. The number of Kanbans is limited, restricting the maximum number of finished items in the system. When a con-
tainer is withdrawn, the accompanying Kanban is detached from the container and placed on the scheduling board. Alternatively, the Kanban may
be detached when the last item is removed from the container or box. Figure 3 shows the basic Kanban system with three different products
(Krieg, 2005).

The original PSO algorithm was inspired by the social behaviour of biological organisms, specifically the ability of groups of some species of ani-
mals to work, as one, in locating desirable positions in a particular area, for example, birds flocking to a food source.

in a uniform random manner throughout the search space; the velocity was also randomly initialized. These are also weighted matrixes rp, rg

Collection of input data
The first step of the method is collection of input data. Generally, the input data are based on the decisions the planner has made in the identifica-
tion of constraints and system design phases, such as the data concern layout and space constraints of the inventory-production system, and the
number of buffer places available at each stop (shop floors: S1, S2, S3, S4, and S5), which is presented in Figure 4.The Milk run area is often called
the SM. It is located between the production line and the central warehouse and allows frequent deliveries of small batches of components.
This set of data is fundamental because it has a direct influence on the planning results and on the system evaluation, which is the last phase
of the complete system planning process. Item-related data are usually collected in a document called the PFEP (Harris, 2003). This document,
which is presented as a table, offers information about every item or part needed for production, logistics, and procurement. Typically, the PFEP
is an exhaustive document. This means that, for every item, an extensive amount of information is available, most of which is not directly related
to the dimensioning phase of the tugger train planning.
The proposed system works in the following way: (a) at the beginning of a production process, the tugger train delivers raw material needed
for production, which are packed in different container types (small boxes, medium boxes, big boxes, and spools) according to the PFEP (Table 1.),
from central warehouse to SM (Figure 4); (b) from the SM, the tugger train delivers each item or part needed for production to the shop floors
(S1, S2, S3, S4, and S5 in Figure 4),where raw materials are stored according to the PFEP; (c) when the production process starts, raw materials
are used and the number of stored containers decreases; (d) the finished goods are made on the last shop floor, that is, S5; (e) using each raw
material container generates a trigger signal, which then calculates raw material replenishment after every trigger when Kanban system is gener-
ated; (f) then the calculation shows the number of raw material containers which are required on the shop floor on which the Kanban trigger is
generated; (g) the raw material containers are prepared and loaded on to the next tugger train; (h) the tugger train leaves the SM and moves along
the defined trajectory stopping on each shop floor (S1, S2, S3, S4, and S5), where it is necessary to unload raw material containers; (i) raw material
containers are unloaded on the last shop floor S5, and the finished goods are picked up; (j) the tugger train goes to the SM, where finish goods get

Experimental results for the Milk run and Kanban systems
According to the nature of PSO, the experiment is repeated 100 times with the data collection and experimental results to optimize the
inventory-production system for minimizing the total available capacity. In particular, (a) the inventory capacity in super market, (b) the capacity in
tugger train transportation, and (c) material stock level on shop floor should be minimized; furthermore, a smooth inventory-production process
should be achieved.
In this research, the term iteration presents the Milk Run tact per train in 20 minutes. The Inventory analysis for 30 Iterations for only three raw
materials, Material 2, Material 6, and Material 9, is presented in Table 3.
Table 3 presents the Milk run and Kanban inventory-production system in 30 iterations. It should be noted that there are different numbers of Kan-
ban cards for different products, thereby indicating that, at the beginning of a production process, there are different numbers of containers on different
shop floors. The table presents the number of Kanban cards (containers), stock level in shop floor; raw material replenishment; and cumulative consumption
until the trigger level. The trigger level after every iteration increases, for every raw material, and when it reaches the Trigger point, the Kanban system
generates information that the existing shop floor needs raw material replenishment—new container(s)—which is marked as YELLOW in Table 3.
After every iteration, usually, some Trigger points are generated. At that moment, the workers in the SM start to prepare the needed raw
material containers. One iteration is necessary for preparation of the raw material containers, and the next iteration is used for the delivery of the
raw material containers. After the Kanban trigger point is reached, two iterations are necessary for delivering the raw material replenishment con-
tainer to the Kanban shop floor. This point is marked GREEN in Table 3. The entire inventory-production process continues from Trigger point,
that is, SM preparation of raw material containers, loading of the raw material containers on tugger train trailers in the SM, transportation of the
raw materials, delivery of the containers to the Kanban shop floor, which generates the Kanban Trigger point, and picking-up of finished goods
from the last shop floor.

CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
A biological swarm intelligence model comprising particle swarm optimization for modelling material flow optimization using Milk run and Kanban
systems in automotive industry is presented in this paper. The proposed models optimize the inventory-production system for minimizing the
total available capacity by (a) minimizing inventory capacity in the supermarket, (b) minimizing capacity in tugger train transport, and (c) minimizing
material stock level on shop floor. At the same time, a smooth inventory-production process is achieved.
The new modified PSO model for the inventory-production system in a unique optimization model is presented in this paper. The major modifi-
cation to the original PSO is using the capacity of a container instead of a velocity component. For each Kanban trigger, the total timing for the Milk
run delivery for the necessary raw materials, capacity of every shop floor, and capacity of the trailers of the tugger train are calculated. In the pro-
posed system, the capacity of the container and the quantity of material in circulation are decreased, which presents significant inventory reduction.
Experimental results encourage further research. On one hand, the proposed system could be improved by using soft computing techniques,
which could stabilize deviation of the number of trails, or changing the number of Kanban cards (containers at the beginning of a production pro-
cess) and improving the inventory-production process. The optimization method, PSO, has several parameters that determine its behaviour and
efficacy. Future studies could explore how to calculate the initial value of iteration for this optimization model and which new constraints could
be added in this specific production process. Future work could focus on extending research on good choice of parameters to various optimization
scenarios, which should help production managers to achieve different solutions and make better operational solutions and achieve production
results with little effort.
