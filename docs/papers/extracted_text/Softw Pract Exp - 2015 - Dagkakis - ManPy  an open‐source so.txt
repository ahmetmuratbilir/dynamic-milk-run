ManPy: an open-source software tool for building discrete event
simulation models of manufacturing systems
Georgios Dagkakis*,†, Ioannis Papagiannopoulos and Cathal Heavey
Enterprise Research Centre, University of Limerick, Limerick, Ireland
SUMMARY
In this paper, we present a new open-source (OS) software library for building discrete event simulation ob-
jects with focus on manufacturing environments. ManPy stands for ‘Manufacturing in Python’ but employs
a generic approach that can be extended to other types of business processes such as services, logistics and
supply chain management. It is written in Python and makes use of the SimPy library to implement a pro-
cess interaction world view. The goal in developing ManPy is to provide an expandable OS layer of well-
deﬁned manufacturing objects, which can be used by users with multiple levels of expertise in discrete event
simulation, namely, a super user and an industrial engineer. This object repository follows a structured ar-
chitecture, allowing developers to extend it, exchange ideas and methodologies, with the goal of forming an
OS community. We explain how ManPy is developed on SimPy, present its architecture and give examples
of its utilization. We also give insight of how this work is planned to progress in order to attract software
developers, modellers and practitioners in an OS community. Copyright © 2015 John Wiley & Sons, Ltd.

1. INTRODUCTION
Discrete event simulation (DES) is arguably one of the most widely accepted and used operations
research (OR) methodologies [1–3]. Although DES is traditionally computationally expensive [4,
5], it is often preferred to mathematical methods due to its ﬂexibility [6–8]. Indeed, in a review
of simulation papers published in the literature of 281 peer-reviewed papers published between
1997 and 2006 [9], researchers found that DES was the most popular OR technique used.
Commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) DES software has been around for several decades and has
contributed considerably to the diffusion of DES in the academic and industrial communities.
The strength of such packages lies in the fact that they provide the user with graphical tools for
modelling, debugging and experimentation, with advanced visual facilities that enhance model de-
velopment [10, 11]. On the other hand, such software introduces multidimensional cost that deters
companies and especially small and medium enterprises from adopting DES-based solutions in their
decision support [12]. Moreover, the lack of access to the source code often hinders important
modelling aspects such as ﬂexibility and reusability [13].
Given the previous issues with COTS tools, an open-source (OS) DES library could provide a
competent comparative tool, providing an open and lower-cost tool, if tailored to be used by users
with multiple levels of expertise in DES. From our review of the state of the art in OS DES [14],
which is brieﬂy presented in Section 2 of this current paper, we found that most tools are targeted
towards users with high programming expertise. Our objective was not only to produce a functional

In this paper, we describe the ManPy library built using Python and SimPy. The main design chal-
lenge in developing ManPy is to encode ManPy objects from the processes used in SimPy models.
These objects can then be used by DES modellers in developing simulation models, in a similar
manner to users of COTS DES tools. In our design, we create an application programming interface
(API) for the creation of extendable OS DES components. ManPy as a simulation engine is modular
so it can be integrated to a wide set of decision support platforms. In this work, we aim to research if
it is possible for expert users and engineers to work with this DES library. To our knowledge, this is
the ﬁrst OS DES library designed to research this hypothesis, and this is a focal scientiﬁc contribu-
tion of the ManPy library.
ManPy is part of a broader OS simulation-based decision support platform being developed
within the FP7 project titled ‘Decision support in Real-time for Efﬁcient Agile Manufacturing’
(DREAM, http://dream-simulation.eu/ accessed 2 February 2015) which started in October of
2012. ManPy is the simulation engine being built within the DREAM platform, and it constitutes
a standalone project. The DREAM code, including ManPy, is accessible at the project repository
in GitHub (https://github.com/nexedi/dream accessed 2 February 2015).
The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: in Section 2, we provide a more in-depth
discussion on the motivation for the development of the OS library. In Section 3, we give a brief
presentation of SimPy, the OS library on which the ManPy OS tool has been developed and also
present the overall design for ManPy. This will allow the reader to understand what SimPy offers
and comprehend why ManPy is a separate project. Section 4 contains a presentation of ManPy.
We start by explaining the design and architecture of the tool and proceed giving two examples
of models that use ManPy objects. In Section 5, we describe the approach used for code veriﬁcation
and consistency establishment between different versions. Next, in Section 6, we present an over-
view of three case studies where the tool is being utilized. Finally, Section 7 is a discussion about
how an OS DES project targeted at manufacturing can reach users of different levels and form a
community that develops the object repository. We conclude with a summary of the presented ma-
terial and notes on the future steps of our developmental process.
2. MOTIVATION
The ﬁrst step in our study was to conduct a review of the state of the art in OS DES. Our review [14]
covered scientiﬁc literature and OS projects that deal with DES. Our search revealed 44 OS DES
projects. In order to be able to evaluate them and extract information on the state of the art in OS
DES, we deﬁned nine evaluation criteria. These were as follows:
• Language: the general purpose language that the tool is written in.
• License: the OS license that the tool is distributed under.
• Latest release: the time that the latest version of the software was released.
• Latest update: the time that the project was last updated in any way.
• Simulation technique: we reviewed DES tools, but some of them combined DES with another
technique, for example, agent-based simulation, so we noted this.
• Domain: the domain of problems that the tool is focused on, communications, computing,
manufacturing and so on.
• Documentation: means that the project provides for documentation. These can include docu-
ments, online tutorials, videos and so on.
• Communication: means that the project uses to facilitate the communication between users.
• Version control: the means, if any, that the project uses in order to manage different versions of
the code and support documents.
This work led to numerous interesting results. First of all, the large number of projects shows that
there is ongoing interest in the domain. However, most of the projects seem to be abandoned or at
least fail to attract a community of users; this is evidenced by examining archives of communication
portals, such as mailing lists or fora. Another deﬁciency we identiﬁed is that many projects seem to

Another interesting ﬁnding was that in the domain of modelling of computer and telecommuni-

The previous analysis provides motivation to research the development of an OS DES tool that
would be focused on supporting engineers but also have modelling ﬂexibility to allow the library
to be embedded into decision support systems by software experts. More speciﬁcally, we identiﬁed
two potential categories of users for an OS DES engine:
• Super user (SU): he/she can access the code directly, customize objects ﬂexibly or create
completely new ones and embed these simulation objects within decision support systems.
He/she needs to have good coding and modelling skills.
• Industrial engineer (IE): he/she can use tailored objects in order to connect them and create a
model. Also, he/she may apply some code to customize the behaviour of the model within
certain boundaries. Limited coding experience is required at this level, but good modelling
skills are essential.
Two options were available to develop an OS DES tool for manufacturing: (i) develop a new
library or (ii) adopt an existing library. From examining existing libraries, the three most competent
projects we identiﬁed were OMNeT++ [16, 22], JaamSim [23] and SimPy [24].
OMNeT++ (http://www.omnetpp.org/ accessed 2 February 2015) is arguably the most successful
OS DES project found. It has an active community of users and has been used in numerous cases
and publications (http://www.omnetpp.org/publications accessed 2 February 2015). As we already
stated, it was also used as the basis of other tools [18, 25]. OMNeT++ is primarily targeted to the
modelling of computer and telecommunication networks. It is generic and well implemented, but
whereas there have been numerous models and extensions regarding speciﬁc issues and protocols
of computer and telecommunication networks, we could ﬁnd few applications in the domains of
manufacturing or logistics [26, 27]. Bause et al. [28] also report that they could not ﬁnd much re-
search available about analysis of business processes using the OMNeT++ platform. Moreover,
they claim that the mapping of their hierarchical process chains models onto OMNeT++ has been
feasible, yet far from being trivial, because complex hierarchies had to be transferred from one view
into another view. The second, and maybe most important, reason that would make OMNeT++ in-
compatible with our needs is its non-permissive license. As it is stated in the OMNeT++ website,
the free Academic Public License covers the use of the tool only in academic and educational en-
vironments, while a commercial license is required for commercial users.
JaamSim (http://jaamsim.com/ accessed 2 February 2015) is a tool developed by the Canadian
consulting company Ausenco (http://www.ausenco.com/ accessed 2 February 2015). Although they
have been working on their simulation engine for more than a decade [13], the project was released
as OS only in 2013 [23]. JaamSim is equipped with an impressive 3-D user interface that can com-
pete against COTS DES software. The project gets daily updates and maintains a forum with sev-
eral topics. Another asset is that JaamSim as software can be downloaded as a standalone
executable (of about 6 to 7MB for different distributions regarding Windows, OS X and Linux).

SimPy (http://simpy.readthedocs.org/ accessed 2 February 2015) is a DES framework based on

community of users that communicate mostly via a dedicated mailing list. Documentation is also

models. SimPy can be used as a low-level simulation language, which provides easiness in the

One of the most important reasons that we selected SimPy has been the fact that it is written in
Python. As we explained, the aspiration of our work is to develop a library of manufacturing objects
that users can customize and exchange at different levels. In order for this library to remain actively
updated, it would have to attract modellers who would utilize it, primarily through parameterization
and addition of some Python code to create their own objects. As we already stated, DES modelling
is challenging [32] and especially modelling of manufacturing systems, which requires a back-
ground of industrial engineering. People with such background are often not experts in software en-
gineering. Python provides an intuitive syntax that makes it easier for modellers to write code [33].
This is one of the reasons that engineers have started to consider it as a strong free alternative to
MATLAB [34] with Python being actively used in engineering, extended with specialized libraries
[15]. In this aspect, a simulation engine written in Python can easily be integrated with other popular
Python scientiﬁc libraries like SciPy [35] or scikit-learn [36] or even simulation-oriented tools like
ScipySim [37], which facilitates heterogeneous simulation, and PySimulator [38], which provides an
OS environment to perform simulations with different simulation engines and analysis plug-ins.
Python possesses also speciﬁc attributes that enhance the writing of a simulation programme for a
user with software expertise. Namely, its ﬂexible type casting facilitates the building of an environ-
ment that contains instances of classes that communicate like black boxes. Moreover, Python offers
numerous decorators and methods for effective list processing, which is a core element of the DES
paradigm. It is not implied that other computer languages do not provide efﬁcient methods for these
issues, but the easiness that Python offers in this aspect makes it more attractive even to users that
are not computer experts.
The trade-off is that Python, as a scripting language, is by default slower than static languages
such as C++ or Java [39]. There have been attempts to enhance the performance of Python gener-
ally [40, 41] and SimPy speciﬁcally [42]. Cloud deployment of simulation in a simulation-as-a-
service (SimaaS) approach [43] can be another solution for a cloud-enabled OS simulation project.
3. SIMPY AND MANPY OVERALL DESIGN
This section is a brief presentation of the SimPy project. The scope is to provide the reader with an
understanding of what SimPy is and how ManPy is a different project built on the SimPy library.
SimPy stands for ‘Simulation in Python’ and is a package for process-oriented DES written
exclusively in Python. SimPy’s design offers a clean way of handling Python’s generators [24].
A normal function or method in Python (and most programming languages) executes its code from
the ﬁrst line every time it is called. If the method needs to return a value, then the return keyword is
used after which the method exits. The next time that the method is called, its execution will com-
mence again from the beginning of its code. A generator method in Python is declared like any
other method. The difference is that it can make use of the yield command in order to return a value
and release control. When the method is invoked again, it will not execute its code from the begin-
ning, but from the next line of the last yield statement that released control.
Python generators have many uses in large calculations but are also ideal for coding processes
that run in parallel in a DES environment. In a process-oriented world view, processes like

SimPy employs generators cleanly in order to give the means for the user to write simulations. In
every model, an instance from the Environment class is created. This is where all the simulation
objects reside and can interact with each other. Then SimPy offers two major classes [24]:
• Process: models an entity that evolves in simulation time. It is not a formal Python thread but
allows processes to run in parallel in the simulation environment.
• Resource: models an entity that other entities may compete for (e.g. a server).
SimPy has an active community of users who communicate through a dedicated mailing list. The
code is hosted in Bitbucket (https://bitbucket.org accessed 2 February 2015) and mercurial (http://
mercurial.selenic.com/ accessed 2 February 2015), which are utilized for version control. The pro-
ject gets frequent commits, almost on a daily basis, while a new intermediate version (e.g.
SimPy3.0.1) is released on a monthly frequency. Documentation on the website is adequate, giving
an overview of the language and several results.
SimPy has been around as a project for more than a decade. However, the new SimPy3 version
was released last year and introduced many changes in the syntax, rendering models written in
SimPy2 not compatible with the new release. There are directions on how to port code from one
version to the other, but from our experience, it is not always straightforward. We consider the fact
that the project did not keep its backwards compatibility for consistency. Nonetheless, the new ver-
sion does offer more ﬂexibility. For example, in SimPy2, the programmer had to explicitly deﬁne
processes, while in a SimPy3 environment, everything is regarded as a process and can act as such.
Furthermore, SimPy3 allows the deﬁnition of more than one generator method in the same object,
which does enhance pliability in coding.
From our interaction with SimPy, we established that it is a solid package, which allows clean
coding of simulations. On the other hand, the user has to be a coder even for very simple problems.
Even from the very ﬁrst example that SimPy community offers (http://simpy.readthedocs.org/en/
latest/examples/ accessed 2 February 2015), the user has to deﬁne process functions and deal with
Python keywords such as ‘with’ and ‘yield’ that may bafﬂe even experienced Python programmers.
Using SimPy is a big step over using plain Python for simulations, because the parallel nature of the
task is highly enhanced. However, the fact that SimPy is not focused on giving prefabricated
simulation components, like most COTS software alternatives do, makes it difﬁcult for non-
experienced coders to use. This is the gap that ManPy aspires to ﬁll for manufacturing environ-
ments; however, ManPy has been developed in a generic manner to allow it to be used in other
areas, for example, modelling of health care systems. Figure 1 gives a graphical depiction of the
semantic transition from SimPy to ManPy.
This section has given a brief introduction to SimPy for the requirements of this article. For more
information on this project, the reader is referred to its website that offers several documents.
Additionally, Matloff [24, 44] provides an excellent tutorial on SimPy. Unfortunately, it is written
for SimPy2, so examples are not applicable to the new version and of course it does not cover new
features. Nevertheless, it still provides valuable information for DES coding in Python and SimPy.
4. MANPY
As we already stated, ManPy stands for ‘Manufacturing in Python’ and is a library of manufactur-

describe how ManPy is built and how it can be utilized to model manufacturing systems. We start
explaining our design decisions. Then we describe the architecture of ManPy code in a class
hierarchy. We continue by giving a number of examples of how ManPy objects can be used and
also customized.

4.1. Design
The scope behind ManPy’s design was to offer objects, that is, classes consisting of code, which a
user will perceive in a similar way he views the objects he/she drags and drops in COTS simulation
software tools that implement the process interaction world DES view [45]. Simulation objects that
a user usually sees in the animated GUI of COTS software can be classiﬁed semantically into three
major categories:
• Permanent objects that have a ﬁxed position in the model. These are mostly servers of any
kind, buffers and entry or exit points.
• Permanent objects that move in the model. These are resources that other objects compete for.
So, for example, an operator can be a permanent resource in a production line, but at a given
simulation time, he may work on different stations.
• Temporary objects that move in the model. These enter the model at some point of the simu-
lation time, get buffered and processed in different stations and then exit the system. They may
model parts getting processed in a production line, customers in a super market, patients in a
clinic and so on.
Most DES software regard all of the previous categories as processes in its internals [46]. This is
also the typical use of SimPy [24] that would consider both a customer and a cashier as a process.
Nevertheless, an IE who uses proprietary simulation software gets ﬁrstly acquainted with the per-
manent objects, which he or she drags and drops in a model window and connects them with arrows
to deﬁne the ﬂow of temporary objects in the model. The ﬁrst advanced customizations that a user
typically tries are in permanent objects, that is, servers. For example if a modeller wants to extend
the default behaviour of the customer when he or she reaches a server, he or she would do it through
the server. In Plant Simulation [47], this would be most probably by adding a method in the
Entrance control of SingleProc that is the Plant basic element to model servers. In Simul8
(http://www.simul8.com/ accessed 2 February 2015), one would also change the Routing in actions
of an Activity, which is the basic Simul8 component to model servers. It is indicative that in both
software packages, the user, in order to override default behaviour, has to write some code.

ManPy is designed to offer a similar world view in an OS coding environment. The user gets
prefabricated components that he or she is able to connect as black boxes in order to form a model.
Of course, taking ManPy as a standalone project, the user would still have to create the model
programmatically. But, as we will see, no coding of functions or methods should be needed, just
declaration of the instances and their connections and also simulation parameters (e.g. length of
an experiment). Furthermore, because the objects are modular, the simulation engine is easily
integrated with any third-party user interface; this allows the user to deﬁne the model graphically
and create the ManPy conﬁguration in the background.
The objects implement a well-deﬁned API of methods so that an advanced user can easily cus-
tomize their behaviour in speciﬁc occasions, for example, at the event of a moving object entering
into a server as stated in the previous text. A more advanced user can follow the conventions of the
API to code completely new objects and incorporate them into the platform.
So, the design is inspired by COTS simulation software, yet it implies some basic differences.
The platform is targeted at the provision of prefabricated simulation components, but this is
performed in a completely OS framework rendering the capability of populating the repository
and exchanging objects. Of course, the difﬁculty here is to provide means of describing an object’s
speciﬁc logic so that it can really become usable to a third party. Another difference to COTS is that
ManPy is a standalone simulation library. Even though it can be connected to a GUI or some other
module, it has no dependency with those. In this aspect, it is more ﬂexible in interfacing with soft-
ware or being deployed on the Web.
4.2. Architecture
Discrete event simulation is traditionally an appropriate paradigm where object-oriented (OO) pro-
gramming can be used [48]. It is indicative that the ALGOL-based simulation language Simula was
historically the ﬁrst that introduced the class concept [49]. In order to achieve the goals set in the
design, ManPy needs to implement a structured OO architecture.
ManPy architecture is depicted in Figure 2. As we see, the high-level architecture consists of four
layers. The basis consists of pre-existing technology that includes Python, SimPy and other Python
libraries such as NumPy [15] that is used for numerical computations or xlrd and xlwt [35] that
allow reading from and writing into spreadsheet (xls) formats. It is important to state that the ManPy
setup handles all these dependencies. So, a user of a Python-enabled computer can instal ManPy
and its dependencies at once.
Then, there is a layer of abstract ManPy classes. A generic ManPyObject is the one that all ManPy
simulation objects inherit from. These simulation objects are grouped into four abstract classes:

• CoreObject: all the permanent stations of the model. These can be servers or buffers of any
type and also entry and exit points.
• ObjectInterruption: all the objects that can affect the availability of another object. For ex-
ample, failures, scheduled breaks and shifts.
• Entity: all objects that get processed by or wait in CoreObjects and that are not permanent in a
model. For example, parts in a production line, customers in a shop and calls in a call centre.
• ObjectResource: all the resources that might be necessary for certain operations. For example,
repairman, operator and electric power. An ObjectResource is necessary in modelling when
two or more CoreObjects compete for the same resource (e.g. two machines competing for the
same operator).
Additionally, we have one more category in the abstract level that we denote as Auxiliary. These
are not simulation objects and therefore do not inherit from the ManPyObject, but classes and
methods needed for different simulation or coding functionalities. For example, a class enhances
random number and random variable generation. Unlike the other categories described here, auxil-
iary components are not necessary classes nor do they inherit from one parent class. This category is
more of a grouping, so we describe this relationship with dashed arrows.
By the term ‘abstract’, we mean that these classes are not meant to be instantiated. CoreObject is an
infrastructure that provides basic methods to build objects like machines to be used in a model. How-
ever, the class itself is not designed to be used in a model as is. The same applies to ObjectInterruption,
Entity and ObjectResource. This architecture is an attempt to abstract the simulation model into a set of
CoreObjects that exchange Entities. We refer to this exchange process as ‘transaction’. Both
CoreObjects and Entities may need ObjectResources for certain operations, and ObjectInterruptions
may affect the availability of an object at a given simulation time. Auxiliary classes and methods help
in creating and executing simulation models and interfacing with other software.
In the next level, ManPy objects extend the abstract classes to model a speciﬁc functionality.
These classes can be used in a simulation model. Because these objects have to be able to interact
like black boxes, ManPy offers an API of methods to model the behaviour of the object. It is out of
the scope of the paper to give a complete list of this API. The reader is referred to ManPy documen-
tation in GitHub. As an example, we list in the succeeding text the categorization of methods the
CoreObjects implement:
i. Deﬁnition methods: are used for the instantiation of the object; for example, deﬁneRouting
deﬁnes how the object is connected with other CoreObjects.
ii. Signalling methods: objects communicate sending signals to one another. These methods
handle the communication; for example, signalReceiver will send a signal to the receiver in
a transaction.
iii. Transaction methods: are used to deﬁne how the objects exchange Entities; for example,
getEntity is invoked each time a CoreObject receives an Entity.
iv. State control methods: are used to retrieve the state of an object; for example, canAccept
returns true if the object is in the state to receive an entity. This may follow a different logic
for different types of objects. For example, a standard server can accept entities only if it is
idle, a standard buffer only if not all of its capacity is taken or a standard exit point that is able
to accept an Entity.
v. Flow control methods: used to control the ﬂow of information and more speciﬁcally route the
entities through the network of objects. For example, selectReceiver is a static method that
chooses from a list of possible receivers for an Entity that the CoreObject holds and is in
the state to dispose.
vi. Supplementary methods: are used to deﬁne certain objects in transaction or control methods.
For example, getGiverObject returns the giver in a transaction.
vii. Output and calculation methods: are used either to output results or trace in different formats
or to make certain calculations. For example, calculateProcessingTime returns the processing
time that a server needs for an entity. In a standard Machine, this may be modelled by a dis-
tribution. In a job-shop environment though, a Machine will have to deﬁne the processing time
depending on the attributes of the Entity being processed.

4.3. Basic ManPy objects
In the Auxiliary Abstract Class is the main script, which is used to develop a DES model. The
ManPy main script performs the following operations:
1. reads or deﬁnes the objects;
2. creates the objects;
3. deﬁnes the structure and sets the topology of the model;
4. in every replication:
a. initializes the simulation, the objects and sets the work in progress;
b. activates the objects;
c. runs the simulation; and
d. conducts post-processing operations; and
5. after the simulation is over, outputs the results in a desirable way.
A main script is separate from the simulation objects used within a DES model, so users may
deﬁne their own main script, which may constitute interfaces with other software, for example,
an excel spreadsheet or a drag-and-drop GUI. Main scripts can also be small pieces of raw Python
code like the examples documented in the succeeding text.

# sort the receivers according to their priority
possibleReceivers.sort(key=lambda x: x.priority, reverse=True)
if possibleReceivers[0].canAccept():

elif possibleReceivers[1].canAccept():
return possibleReceivers[1]

5. CODE VERIFICATION
According to Shannon [1], ‘Veriﬁcation seeks to show that the computer programme performs as
expected and intended’. Simulation code is notoriously difﬁcult to debug. In the ManPy world view
(and in other DES programmes), we have processes moving in parallel, obtaining and suspending
control of the programme at certain points of the simulation time. These transitions are triggered
by events that in even simple models it is impossible for the developer to fully review. So, evalu-
ating the code accuracy becomes extremely complex.
A primary means of veriﬁcation is the provision of methods to output the simulation trace. Every
ManPy object can implement its version of outputTrace, which outputs signiﬁcant events of the
simulation run that can be stored in a spreadsheet. All the objects output to the same sheet, and
the events are sorted according to simulation time. The trace is used for debugging and veriﬁcation
of very simple models. For more complex cases, more robust means are required. In the succeeding
text, we present methods we use to establish the consistency of ManPy code.
5.1. Against COTS
Commercial off-the-shelf simulation software has been around for several decades. These packages
exist in a highly competitive environment, continuously striving to improve their products at all
levels. Also, they currently have more industrial users than most OS alternatives; thus, they get
wider testing.
Every time we build a novel ManPy object or model, we compare the results against a COTS
alternative. The tool we use is Plant Simulation by Siemens [47]. Plant is a powerful package that
provides the user with a highly ﬂexible OO environment. In the left part of Figure 9, we see the
model of our second example as it was built in Plant Simulation. The EndSim method was used
to output the results at the end of the simulation as shown in the right part of the ﬁgure. In such
deterministic cases, it is possible for us to establish the full accuracy of the code. Even for results
that include many decimals, for example, the working ratio of a machine, we need the models to
give identical results up to the last decimal.
In stochastic situations, of course, we cannot achieve the same convergence. Python uses the
Mersenne Twister [50] as its core random number generator (RNG). In Plant Simulation, we could
not identify which RNG is employed. Even if both two simulation software worked with the exact
same RNG and seeds, they may not give identical results. The reason is that the same system may
be modelled with slightly different logic in two different simulation engines. For this reason, ran-
dom numbers may be produced in a different order, leading to different results.
The way to verify such models is to use conﬁdence intervals. As an example, we change the pro-
cessing time of a Machine in the ManPy model we described so that it follows the normal distribu-
tion. We set the average to 0.25 and the standard deviation to 0.01. We also truncate the distribution

5.2. Unit testing
Unit testing [52] is used in ManPy in order to establish the code’s consistency in different versions.
Simulation software is complex, and sometimes, a change in part of the code introduces errors that
the developer cannot foresee. For this reason, each time a model is adequately veriﬁed (using the
methods described in the previous text) its result is added in a test dump folder. A speciﬁc test Py-
thon script can automatically run all the tests that are already committed in the project. In essence,
this script sequentially runs all the test simulation models and compares their results to the expected
ones. For every test, there are three possible outcomes: error that means the code crashed, fail that
means the code did not crash but the result was different than the expected and ok that means the
model gave the expected results (see Figure 10). In the case of errors, the reason is also outputted
in the console at the end of the test.

In deterministic situations, unit testing is straightforward. In stochastic ones, we can still achieve
the expected results if we statically deﬁne the random number generation seed of the model in the
input. However, we found that in different platforms and operation systems, the random generation
process of Python may vary, leading to failure of the test. This is a problem we try to overcome by
relaxing the stochastic tests not to expect speciﬁc results but results that lie within intervals.
The unit test checks that the pre-existing code runs as expected. However, every time a new ob-
ject is incorporated into the platform, there is no initial test coverage for it. So, the developer has to
verify it on his/her own and then create one or more tests so that this object is also tested in the fu-
ture. As a result, the number of tests increases over time. Currently, ManPy employs more than 150
tests that run in about 1min (depending on the computer system). What is important is that the de-
veloper can promptly gain some conﬁdence that his changes did not affect already veriﬁed code. In
order to run the tests or add new ones in the dump, only one command in the shell is required.
After all the unit tests are successful, a change is committed in the version control system. In the
development, we use Git (http://git-cm.com/ accessed 4 November 2014) for version control. This
provides a solid infrastructure for developers to work in parallel, keeps track of changes in different
versions and provides support in being able to recognize the cause of potential problems.
6. CASE STUDIES
In our review of OS DES [14], we found that one of the main deﬁciencies of the domain is that pro-
jects rarely get to be utilized in real-life industrial cases. As we stated in the introduction, ManPy
has been developed within a project called DREAM. The project offered pilot cases provided by
industrial partners. The purpose of this paper is to present ManPy as a generic simulation engine
without focusing on speciﬁcs of a model. For this reason, detailed description of the cases is sched-
uled for future publication work. Nevertheless, in this section, we give insight on how real-world
case studies meliorated ManPy development and also why an OS tool can be advantageous in such
cases.

was found ﬂexible in interfacing with optimization routines. This was performed at two different
levels, either calling an optimization routine inside the simulation run or creating an optimization
routine that uses the ManPy model to stochastically evaluate the scenarios it produces. The two ap-
proaches can be employed concurrently as shown in Figure 12. Python gives the asset of easily cod-
ing optimization routines. Moreover, it contains many scientiﬁc libraries that handle such problems.

ciﬁc linear constraints.

large-scale models with additional complexity, which followed real-life requirements. Extensions to
ManPy fell semantically inside the ﬁve echelons of architecture given in Figure 2, providing an ini-
tial proof of its completeness. Also, the fact that pilot cases were covered by objects that inherited
basic ManPy components (e.g. Machine and Queue) demonstrates that the objects are really exten-
sible and reusable.

plex, accommodating several different levels of users [57]. The reasons behind the motivation of
developers to contribute in an OS project, without having monetary compensation, constitute a sub-
ject of scientiﬁc research [58, 59]. The fact that OS is ‘free’ does not mean that the project does not

of computer and telecommunication networks is more mature is also highlighted by the existence of
review and comparison papers in this area [19, 20].
As mentioned in Section 2, ManPy aspires to draw the attention of two levels of users, an SU and
an IE. Most of the projects we ﬁnd seem to consider only the SU level. It is true that advanced users
may also be the main code contributors who extend the platform with new elements; however, the
attraction of IE users is essential for longevity of an OS project in this domain.
In the remainder of this section, we discuss issues in order to attract practitioners (IEs) and devel-
opers (SUs) into an OS DES community and we speculate how ManPy can interact with both.
7.1. Technical infrastructure
One problem that we believe deters developers from getting involved with OS DES is that many
projects fail to successfully adopt and use common OS practices. One indicative example is that
in several OS projects, we could not ﬁnd the license under which they are distributed [14]. That
would easily discourage a developer in his ﬁrst interaction with a project. Also, many lack docu-
mentation or means of communication between the users. Finally, several do not employ popular
OS practices like version control systems or bug trackers. Fogel [62] describes many critical factors
for the success of an OS project.
ManPy is hosted in GitHub and is licensed under the terms of the Lesser General Public License.
A brief readme ﬁle in the root of the repository informs the user about the scope of the project, its
current status and how to get started. Then, ManPy documentation guides him through the ﬁrst steps
of installing and using the code. The Git version control system is adopted in order to facilitate dis-
tributed development. As we already described in the previous section, we have developed a testing
mechanism in order to establish the consistency of the code between different versions.
The previous are primary steps that help us achieve efﬁcient development as long as the team of
contributors is small. Nonetheless, there are several other issues to consider for the future. First of
all, documentation has to be actively updated and separated for different issues and users. Also, more
means than traditional Portable Document Format (PDF) documents should be considered. Simulation
is a traditionally complex technique [32], and the use of simulation software, no matter how well
written, imposes an initial learning curve. Interactive online prototypes that allow the user to manipu-
late simple models and examine the results make it possible to easily comprehend the usability of the
software. An online ManPy module is currently being designed, in order to help IEs to learn the tool.
Installation and deployment is often cumbersome in OS. ManPy is exclusively written in Python
and uses other Python libraries like SimPy, xlwt to be able to output trace in xls format or NumPy
[63] for scientiﬁc calculations. These may be dependent to other software. For example, NumPy de-
pends on the ATLAS and BLAS highly optimized linear algebra packages. Both ATLAS and
BLAS are written in FORTRAN, so one needs a FORTRAN compiler tool-chain to build those li-
braries. Furthermore, the build process is different depending on the underlying operating system
used. The task of manually downloading the proper versions along with building and installing
those libraries is a cumbersome and error-prone process that can be automated. An alternative
would be to supply some ready-to-use software in the form of virtual machine images or software
installers (executive ﬁles) with all included, but these distributions would still have to be packaged.

7.2. Object repositories
The primary goal for ManPy is to create a layer of prefabricated objects and methods so that users
can build simulation models. This is not much different than what most COTS simulation software
offers. Nevertheless, what can make ManPy advantageous is that this repository of objects can be
expanded at different levels. No matter how mature, the prefabricated objects that simulation soft-
ware provides are never enough to easily model a real system, and additional coding is always
needed. One could populate the software with more objects implementing speciﬁc logic. However,
this would lead to a large number of simulation objects that would increase the learning curve and
make the use of the software complex.
One way to confront this is to make a distinction in the granularity of different levels of object
repositories. As its name implies, ManPy is primarily focused on manufacturing, but it is
semantic-free, providing means for modelling other domains. Figure 13 summarizes our view of
different levels of object repositories. First, we have the infrastructure of a semantic-free simulation
engine. Then, this infrastructure is populated with domain-speciﬁc objects. It is stated that domain-
speciﬁc DES environments can facilitate easier model development [66]. In the following, these re-
positories can be tailored into an instance speciﬁc for an organization. So a company that uses the
software obtains only objects that are tailored to their type of system. Finally, we have the users in-
side the organization that may be IEs who construct models utilizing the tailored objects or end
users that just use a tailored simulation model in their every-day decision support.

models. The goal in developing the ManPy library was to support users in embedding DES with
other decision support platforms and also to support users with multiple levels of expertise (a super
user and an industrial engineer) in DES.

So, issues such as code quality improvement, development of multilevel documentation and attrac-
tion of users are by default ongoing in an OS project that wishes to remain active.

echelon of validation is to check if the code is really usable by a third party. This cannot be per-
formed via the pilot cases that we model, but only if we attract external developers to try to use
ManPy. An online ManPy module is currently being designed, in order to help IEs to learn the tool.

software. The interface of ManPy with other modules uses formatted JSON ﬁles. In addition to
enhancing usability of DREAM, this helps us also to verify the concepts of modularity and inter-
connectivity of our simulation library. This work is currently being validated through the use of
ManPy in the three pilot cases.
