Preface

Globalization, unpredictable markets, increased product customization and the quest for competitive 
advantages are but a few of the many challenges facing manufacturing enterprises now and in the foreseeable 
future. Frequent changes in product, production technologies and manufacturing systems are evident today 
along with their significant implementation costs. Increased competitiveness requires enhanced quality and 
value through increasingly innovative products, materials and production technologies.  One key strategy for 
manufacturing success and economic sustainability is to satisfy the market demand for increased product 
variants while reducing the resulting variations in their manufacturing and associated cost and complexity. This 
trend is on the rise as evidenced by the paradigm shifts witnessed in manufacturing systems and their increased 
flexibility and responsiveness to cope with the evolution of both products and manufacturing systems.  
A host of external and internal change drivers exist that affect manufacturing enterprises at various levels 
from strategic planning for re-positioning the business, to tactical control of production plans and facilities to 
achieve a high degree of responsiveness. The changing manufacturing environment requires careful effort to 
prolong the life of manufacturing systems by making them easily adaptable and facilitating the integration of 
new technologies and new functions. This requires pre-designing effective paths for change and providing 
innovative change enablers and adaptation mechanisms.  
The availability of solutions that go beyond adaptability and flexibility will thus be crucial for enhancing 
productivity and ensuring competitiveness and economic sustainability in the manufacturing sector.  
The theme of the 4th International Conference on Changeable, Agile, Reconfigurable and Virtual production 
(CARV2011) is “Enabling Manufacturing Competitiveness and Economic Sustainability.” The leading edge 
research and best implementation practices and experiences, which address these important issues and 
challenges, presented in the CARV2011conference are included in this book. It is organized in 5 Parts and 
contains 107 papers contributed by 242 authors from around the world. The topics cover leading edge advances 
in manufacturing systems design, planning, evaluation, control and evolving paradigms such as mass 
customization, personalization, changeability, reconfigurability and flexibility. It treats new and important 
concepts such as the dynamic product families and platforms, and co-evolution of products and systems, and 
methods for enhancing manufacturing systems’ economic sustainability and prolonging their life for use to 
produce more than one product generation. Enablers of change in manufacturing systems, production volume 
and capability scalability and managing the volatility of markets, competition among global enterprises and the 
increasing complexity of products, manufacturing systems and management strategies are discussed. An outline 
of industry challenges and future directions for research and development needed to help both practitioners and 
academicians are presented.

Change in Manufacturing – Research and Industrial Challenges 
H. ElMaraghy, T. AlGeddawy, A. Azab, W. ElMaraghy
 
Intelligent Manufacturing Systems Centre, University of Windsor, ON Canada N9B-3P4 
 
 
Abstract 
Change, in products and systems, has become a constant in manufacturing. This sector continues to witness major market shifts,
introduction of new materials and processing technologies as well as great changes in consumer preferences and products 
variety. This presents significant challenges to industrialists, researchers and educators alike. Changes can most often be 
anticipated but some go beyond the design range. This requires providing innovative change enablers and adaptation 
mechanisms to mitigate the effects of, and capitalize on, changes in manufacturing. Experiential research and learning is
essential to enhance responsiveness and prepare production leaders of the future. The training and experimentation could be 
significantly enhanced if manufacturing systems could be brought into the laboratories of academic and research institutions. 
This paper describes the latest state-of-the art fully reconfigurable “plug & play” changeable and flexible “Factory-in-the-Lab”
infrastructure and supporting design innovation and advanced research environment. It discusses the use of the iFactory and
iDesign system to address these challenges and develop many key technologies and strategies for success. It presents several 
novel approaches and methods for achieving the desired balance in the wide spectrum of variation in markets, products, 
processes, systems and manufacturing enterprises.   
 
Keywords:  
Product Design, Process Design, Manufacturing Systems, Reconfigurable Transformable Factory, Co-Development and 
Synthesis, Learning Factory, Manufacturing Sustainability 
1 
INTRODUCTION 
The manufacturing sector is vitally important to the economic 
prosperity of any nation. Manufacturing output makes up one sixth 
of Canada’s Gross Domestic Product (GDP) and employs more 
than two million people. Over the past several years, this vital 
economic sector has faced significant challenges due to 
globalization, shortage of professional and skilled labour, concern 
about the environment and need for high quality customized 
products at a competitively low cost. There is a clear need for 
innovative product design and manufacturing approaches to ensure  
competitiveness, responsiveness, and sustainability. 
While some manufacturers have responded and performed 
admirably, many have struggled in this changing environment, jobs 
have 
been 
lost 
and 
communities 
have 
suffered. 
Today, 
manufacturers have to continuously upgrade their products, 
processes and technologies simply to maintain their position in the 
market; and the pace of change will continue to accelerate due to 
the 
development 
and 
transformational 
application 
of 
new 
technologies.  
Accelerated 
innovation 
and 
product 
evolution 
also 
bring 
tremendous challenges. This continuous change risks adversely 
affecting product quality and cost. There are also hidden costs in 
the form of response time delays and lost business opportunities 
that 
result 
from 
this 
complex 
changeable 
manufacturing 
environment. Increased complexity of manufacturing systems and 
business is a fundamental residue of globalization and the 
information age. Products design is becoming more complex partly 
due to the coupling of mechanical and computer controls and 
software. This new reality necessitates the development of new 
strategies and enablers for dealing with changeability. While 
change is inevitable, it is important to take advantage of it and make 
it happen efficiently. One key strategy for success is to satisfy the 
market need for product variations and customization, utilizing the 
new technologies, while reducing the resulting variations and 
complexity of manufacturing and associated cost. The required 
agility, flexibility and reconfigurability would not work effectively 
without the development of new methods and physical and logical 
change enablers that support modularity, compatibility, inter-
changeability and scalability.

4 
MAJOR RESEARCH THRUSTS 
Modern manufacturing is experiencing a paradigm shift towards 
more flexibility and reconfigurability (physically and logically) to 
respond quickly and efficiently to changing production requirements 
and market demands. Products are also experiencing progressive 
evolution and this is affecting their manufacturing systems. 
Changeability is defined as the characteristics to economically 
accomplish early and foresighted adjustments of the factory 
structures and processes on all levels including the enterprise, in 
response to change impulses [10]. Changeability is an umbrella 
framework that encompasses many paradigms such as agility, 
adaptability, flexibility and reconfigurability, which are themselves 
enablers of change. 
There are two types of change enablers: hard/physical enablers 
and soft/logical enablers. The “physical/hard” change enablers 
include the physical attributes that facilitate change. These 
characteristics are not limited to the machinery but they also apply 
to the whole factory configuration and layout, physical plants 
infrastructures and buildings. Hardware changes also require major 
changes at the “logical/soft” enablers level, such as the software 
systems used to control individual machines, complete cells, and 
systems as well as to plan individual operations/processes and to 
plan and control the whole production.

adaptive dynamic systems and actual test cases from industry were 
used for verification [13]. This novel approach and models helps 
design and configure manufacturing systems with longer life and 
plan the co-evolution and co-development of products and systems

4.2 
Reconfiguring Process Plans to Minimize Change  
Unpredictable changes of products design and associated 
engineering specifications trigger frequent changes in process 
plans, which often dictate corresponding costly and time consuming 
changes to jigs, fixtures and machinery. Agility and responsiveness 
to change is an important requirement in process planning, which is 
part of the “soft” or “logical” enablers of change in manufacturing as 
the link between products and their processing steps. Current re-
planning or pre-planning methods generate new process plans from 
scratch every time change takes place, which results in production 
delays, high costs and disruptions on the shop floor. 
A novel process planning concept and mathematical programming 
model have been developed to reconfigure plans through insertion 
not sequencing and to optimize the scope, extent and cost of 
reconfiguration, thus overcoming the complexity and flaws of 
existing models. It reconfigures process plans to account for 
changes in parts’ features beyond the scope of original product 
families as they evolve [8]. This innovative approach will be 
extended to new manufacturing processes, and to include set-up 
planning, features clustering and integration with products CAD 
models. Industrial case studies will be used for testing and 
verification. 
4.3 
Changeable Production Planning and Control Enablers  
There is a need, at the shop floor level, to develop models and tools 
for production planning and control to implement the necessary 
changes and ensure their effectiveness. An agile Manufacturing 
Planning and Control (MPC) system design is proposed to respond 
to the changeability of the manufacturing market uncertainty [9] 
using Component Based Software Engineering (CBSE). This 
“Change Ready” MPC system has been applied to aggregate 
planning and forecasting. It will be tested and demonstrated using 
the iFactory. 
Uncertainty associated with managing the dynamic capacity in 
changeable manufacturing is the main source of its complexity [17]. 
A system dynamics approach to model and analyze the operational 
complexity of dynamic capacity in multistage production is being 
developed to capture the stochastic nature of the main sources of 
complexity associated with dynamic capacity. The system dynamics 
model has been extended by Arafa and ElMaraghy [18] to the 
application operations management theory on volume flexibility to 
develop new macro measures for the enterprise manufacturing 
strategy. Short-term sources of flexibility such as inventory or 
capacity buffers may respond to small market fluctuations in 
demand, while large market fluctuations are more controlled by the 
irreversible type of strategy the firm may possess and accumulate 
over longer time periods regarding production capacity capability. 
Industrial enterprises will have to adapt their manufacturing 
capabilities to outperform the evolving industry benchmark.  The 
benchmark evolution speed, either in market(s) or between industry 
members, is affected by the evolving customer preferences and the 
degree of allowed competition governed by policy makers.  The 
effect of complexity sources on capacity scaling levels and backlog 
management and the trade-off between controllability and 
complexity of the dynamic capacity are modeled and guidelines 
developed to mitigate its effects.  
A Multiple Performance Measures Analysis for Market-Capacity 
Integration Policies will be researched using simulation and Design 
of Experiments (DOE) to model and analyze a Make-To-Order 
(MTO) Reconfigurable / changeable system with scalable capacity. 
Unlike classical capacity scaling policies, the proposed hybrid 
capacity scaling policy including delays will be decided using 
multiple performance measures that reflect cost, internal stability 
and responsiveness and the impact of both tactical capacity policies 
and strategic marketing policies on the overall system performance 
[18].

CHANGE-ORIENTED PROCESS DESIGN 
Process design is an important enabler that connects product 
design and definition with the downstream manufacturing planning 
and production as well as manufacturing system design. Process 
planning is also a support function that lies at the heart of the 
production planning and control cycles; it supports the planning 
activities which take place in both ERP (Enterprise Resource 
Planning) and MES (Manufacturing Execution System)- two 
important digital pillars of any of today’s modern enterprises. As can 
be demonstrated by Figure 2, besides ERP and MES, there is also 
Product Lifecycle Management (PLM). Those three main elements 
of any of current state-of-the-art manufacturing digital enterprises 
communicate well through either standard neutral file formats or via 
proprietary plugs and interfaces that are made available by their 
vendors. The overall objective behind such integrable systems is to 
be able to support the product development lifecycle all the way 
from its definition and design, passing through the different process 
and production planning and control support functions all the way to 
final manufacturing, shipment and other supply chain steps. 
Process planning functionality exists in the upstream part of the 
picture (PLM), where mainly micro- and macro-process planning 
are being performed in both the CAD/CAM and Digital 
Manufacturing PLM components respectively (Figure 2). These are 
two distinguished levels of process planning [26]. Macro-level, 
planning 
is 
concerned 
with 
identifying 
the 
manufacturing 
technologies to be used and the main processing tasks and their 
best sequence. Micro-level planning details process parameters, 
required tools and setups, process time and resources [27]. 
In CAD/CAM, the final detailed micro-planning includes toolpath 
generation, definition of process parameters (e.g. cutting patterns, 
cutting speeds, feeds, etc.). Machinability Data Systems or 
Machining handbooks, soon to be available as libraries in 
CAD/CAM systems, help optimize these parametrs. Higher-end 
macro-level and organizational planning functions are increasingly 
made available in Digital Manufacturing software such Delmia 
(Dassault Systeme) and Tecnomatix (Siemens PLM). 
Macro-level process planning is difficult because of its dependence 
on declarative process knowledge including part geometry, tools, 
machine tools, fixtures and technological requirements and also its 
implied time-dependency represented by the order in which the 
given features should be machined/processed. The optimization 
problem involves many criteria, parameters and constraints and is 
known to be NP-hard [28]. Its complexity has increased with the 
paradigm shifts in manufacturing towards customizable, focused 
and agile flexibility. Increased responsiveness is needed to support 
the increasingly varying product mixes.  
A number of approaches to deal with the effects of variations have 
been proposed. Dynamic process planning considers both the static 
and dynamic capabilities of the manufacturing facility. Integration of 
production scheduling and process planning can be used to support 
the dynamicity of shop floor [29]. Accurate description of resources 
and their availability is a key element of adaptive and practical 
process planning [30] . Adaptive process planning is the ability to 
generate a wide range of process plans, including the machine 
routing and production scheduling for a variety of machine settings. 
However, these approaches are limited to addressing changes on 
the manufacturing system level and ignore the real driving source of 
change on the product level due to changes in features, materials, 
variants as well as introduction of new technologies. Non-linear 
process planning entails the generation and ranking of alternative 
plans for a given part prior to production independent of the 
resource status on the shop floor. It is inefficient and impractical as 
it exhaustively generates all possible scenarios. 
 
Figure 2: Process Planning- an important pillar of current digital 
manufacturing enterprises 
 
Hetem [31], Xu et al. [32], Bley and Zenner [33] considered the 
changeability of the current and future manufacturing systems and 
proposed variant-type systems specific to given part families. 
However, this strictly retrieval-based approach does not support the 
introduction of new features into the part family caused by the 
changing demands. Shabaka and ElMaraghy [34] address the 
problem of reconfiguring machine tools to account for these new 
changes on the part level. Song [35] suggests dynamic adaptive 
process planning systems for RMS. He also proposes dynamic 
process planning [36] as a solution for reconfigurable machine 
systems and machine tools, where similarity theory is exploited. 
However, all these approaches do not consider the change and 
evolution of parts and product families and their effect on process 
and prodction plans. Azab and ElMaraghy [8] proposed for the first 
time a model and methodology for reconfiguring process plans 
(RPP) as needed in response to parts/products changes by 
insertion and subtraction instead of sequencing and reordering of 
operations from scratch.  
It is evident that the field of process planning, with the development 
of its computer-aided generative- and variant-based modeling and 
algorithmic means is lagging behind; the gap between Academia 
and PLM industry is quite wide. One of the main goals and research 
directions at the Intelligent Manufacturing Systems (IMS) centre, 
besides developing further the theory and research fronts in the 
field, is to demonstrate  the robustness of the developed models 
and methods and  integrate them with existing CAD/CAM and 
Digital Manufacturing ystems using state-of-the-art professional and 
commercial deployment tools and realistic case studies.

throughout the life of products and manufacturing systems (Figure

added uncertainty and complexity in design, management,

exists between general regional needs and specific individual 
requirements; which affects the strategic planning decisions starting 
from producing product families to products personalization. 
The degree of homogeneity of customer requirements also affects 
the balance of product design architectures between integral and 
modular designs. More integral parts may only require a simple 
linear processes layout; while having modules may necessitate a 
more complex system configuration and processes layout which 
delay the points of product differentiation to realize efficient mass 
customization. Common unchanged product components are 
candidates for mass production on a dedicated manufacturing 
system, while diverse evolving components may require more 
changeable and adaptable, but more complex and expensive, 
Flexible and Reconfigurable manufacturing systems (FMS and

6.1 
Market Diversity 
There are many diverse market needs : i) general requirements that 
unify the whole market region, ii) more specific, yet common, 
requirements for the different segments of that region, and iii)  more 
individual requirements for different groups of customers (Figure 4). 
Introducing a new portfolio of products [7] to the market involves 
deciding on the degree of commonality or differentiation of features 
between product variants. The objective is to find the right balance 
that maximizes generality and commonality on one hand, and 
increases customer satisfaction, sales and market share on the 
other hand. 
6.2 
Products Design Architecture 
In a modular design; products are composed of modules; whose 
structural elements serve identifiable functions and are strongly 
interconnected, while being relatively weakly connected to elements 
in other modules, and [39]. Modular design architecture helps 
develop new products quickly using alternative modules or module 
instances. Common modules may be shared by different products, 
thus reducing design time and cost [40]. On the other hand; integral 
design has a fixed architecture oriented to an optimized product. In 
the classical coupled product design; changes to one component 
cannot be made without affecting others [41]. However; integral 
architecture has the advantage of simplifying the design by 
reducing the number of parts and, hence, assembly errors, 
uncertainty and complexity, as suggested by the Design for 
Manufacturing and Assembly (DFMA) techniques [42]. 
A design of a portfolio of products that has common and different 
components and parts is a matter of finding the right balance 
between modular and integral architectures; where modularity and 
integration are possible for many product variants, despite their 
conflicting rules. Liaison graphs that show the logical relationships 
between components, and commonality analysis [43] which identify 
areas of commonality and differentiation in design, are essential 
tools for arriving at the desired balance (Figure 5). 
6.3 
Processes Layout Complexity 
Delayed Product Differentiation is a manufacturing strategy used to 
mitigate some of the complexities raised by proliferation of product 
variety and the application of mass customization [44]. However 
such strategy may result in a more complex layout [45]. Conversely, 
the linear constant-pace line is simpler to simulate and operate but 
more difficult to handle due to increased number of product 
variiants [46]. The right balance of linear layout processes platform 
and complex tree-like delayed product differentiation manufacturing 
system present a trade-off between layout simplicity and product 
singularity vs. layout complexity and product variety.

6.4 
Manufacturing Systems Sustainability 
Very low variety products can be always mass produced on a 
dedicated manufacturing system (DMS) taking advantage of the 
economy of scale. However; DMS has fixed capabilities and is not 
adaptable to product changes and variation. Flexible Manufacturing 
Systems have well-defined flexibility scope geared towards a 
specific family of products defined apriori with unchanging 
boundaries [39]. They capitalize on the economy of scope at the 
expense of higher initial investments to accommodate the pre-
defined product variation scope. Reconfigurable Manufacturing 
Systems can handle some changes and differences beyond the 
originally defined family boundaries, it acquires capabilities when 
needed, but it is more complex to design and operate [38]. 
System designers have to maintain the right balance between 
dedication, flexibility and reconfigurability to maximize adaptability 
and changeability and minimize complexity. The iFactory (Figure 6) 
is used to identify the right choice along the wide spectrum of 
manufacturing systems paradigms based on commonality analysis 
in product design and manufacturing processes.

Manufacturing Commonality Footprint 
The new integrated research laboratory iDesign/iPlan/iFactory helps 
find the best balance point in manufacturing. There is a unified 
commonality pattern that relates the different domains of 
manufacturing; a footprint that is almost recurrent (Figure 7).  
Finding the balance between modularity and integration using 
designers’ collaboration and physical models from innovation, 
design and rapid prototyping to test solutions is part of iDesign. The 
trade-off between linear vs. complex processes layout, and defining 
the positions of product differentiation points is examined by 
simulation models in iPlan. Deciding on the dedicated, flexible and 
reconfigurable manufacturing capabilities in the manufacturing 
systems is performed through physical simulation in iFactory.

CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS 
A number of flexibility enablers are being researched and 
developed, as part of the Canada Research Chairs (CRC) Program 
and complemented by NSERC funded research projects and 
industry collaborations to achieve the desired changeability, 
adaptability and responsiveness to the continuously varying 
Change in Manufacturing – Research and Industrial Challenges 


--- SAYFA 20 ---
8
                      
 
 
 
conditions and requirements in manufacturing. Examples of these 
enablers and productivity boosters include new and innovative 
methods for: 1) Reconfiguring process plans and production plans 
(schedules) dynamically and adaptively to respond to changes in 
products features, 2) Developing meta-heuristics to determine the 
desired optimal process plan (localized/RPP or global/optimization) 
by combining the mechanisms of reconfigurable process planning 
with that of sequencing and re-planning, 3) Novel models of co-
evolution of products and systems inspired by those found in nature 
for use in manufacturing systems design and planning of products  
families, platforms and portfolios considering their entire life cycle, 
which will help companies economically extend the utility and life of 
their manufacturing facilities and survive in the current turbulent 
market conditions, 4) New metrics for assessing the changeability 
of manufacturing enterprises and decision support guidelines and 
methods for determining its optimal and economical level to ensure 
their sustainability, and 5) Models that capture the extent of product 
variety and complexity and their effect on the manufacturing and 
assembly systems and their design, operation and complexity and 
optimize the definition of product families and manufacturing 
platforms, and hence reduce their initial and operation cost. 
A collaborative product design and manufacturing system synthesis 
framework is achievable using the described research facilities and 
technologies. A unified model that brings strategic and operational 
planning, in the domains of marketing, product design, process 
planning and system synthesis, is the objective of such 
collaboration. This model can be mathematically formulated using 
the suggested manufacturing commonality footprint, mapping 
together different domains, and verified by the soft and hard 
capabilities of the iDesign/iPlan/iFactory laboratory. 
The results of this research can benefit the automotive industry 
(OEMs and their suppliers), manufacturers of many other products, 
the aerospace industry, etc., all of which are important to the 
economic vitality of the manufacturing sector and have many socio-
economic benefits by creating and maintaining high value jobs and 
enhancing prosperity.
