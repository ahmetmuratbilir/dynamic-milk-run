ABSTRACT 
Manufacturing environments increasingly rely on automation and data-driven decision-making 
to optimize efficiency and production rates. This study explores the application of Discrete 
Event Simulation (DES) to model material flow and predict AGV (Automated Guided Vehicle), 
crane, and cart movements within a factory. The goal is to develop a digital twin that enables 
real-time decision-making, optimizes scheduling, and minimizes bottlenecks. 
 
To achieve this, we utilize SimPy, an open-source Python-based DES library, in conjunction with 
a custom-built API and React.js front-end interface. The study evaluates available DES software 
options and justifies the selection of SimPy based on flexibility, integration capabilities, and its 
suitability for modeling custom business rules. The solution is structured into modular 
components handling path planning, transporters, flows, stations, hot-cold starts, and utilities, 
ensuring adaptability to future improvements. 
 
A validation framework was established, utilizing historical data comparison and real-time 
validation to assess the simulation’s predictive accuracy. Over a 40-day testing period, the 
simulation achieved 89.6% accuracy and a sensitivity, or true positive rate (TPR), of 80.2%. The 
simulation provides a reliable first-pass scheduling tool that can be further refined with 
improved data collection. 
 
 
 
4 
 
The findings indicate that while full automation of AGV deployment is not yet feasible, this 
study lays the foundation for future integration with the factory’s Vehicle Management System 
(VMS). Business implications include the potential for automated scheduling, enhanced 
material flow visibility, and optimization of capacity planning. Future work should focus on 
improving data accuracy, integrating live factory data streams, and refining algorithms for 
predictive scheduling.

Chapter 1: Introduction 
1.1 Motivation and Problem Statement 
The 777X airplanes are the newest members of Boeing's widebody family. This project studies 
the manufacturing process solely at the Composite Wing Center (CWC), where carbon fiber 
wings are produced. As industrial automation becomes more widely adopted, certain 
challenges, such as managing traffic for automated vehicle movement, have emerged in 
advanced factories utilizing these technologies.   
Factories and warehouses employing Automated Guided Vehicles – hereinafter referred to as 
AGVs – face the challenge of configuring AGVs in an integrated environment with a vehicle 
management system – hereinafter referred to as VMS – to ensure the AGVs operate in unison. 
The factory requires the use of AGVs to move material, but these movements are not 
automated and require operator remote-control input, failing to take advantage of the full 
capabilities of AGVs1. Efforts are in place to achieve semi-automation on the factory floor2. 
Automation requires the implementation of a scheduling system3, granting the VMS the ability 
to know exactly when to deploy an AGV, and where it should go. Scheduling moves requires 
understanding the end-to-end process in detail. Additional advantages, such as traffic efficiency 
improvements and automated planning, can be appreciated by the introduction of a well-
designed scheduling system. 
Predicting traffic efficiently requires understanding the complexity of the system. Nonetheless, 
taking a modular approach to developing the simulation allows the mitigation of stochastic 
variability. Several simplifying assumptions must be made to scope a DES project that solves the

Process visibility can improve production by developing new mechanisms, analyzing

Another motivation for this work is the need to implement alternative solutions for problems 
that lack data. Rule-based decision-making is a traditional approach to problems that has now 
been resolved more effectively by machine learning algorithms; however, these models 
become ineffective when substantial amounts of clean, usable data are unavailable. In low-data 
environments, rule-based heuristics can serve as an initial decision-making framework where 
predictive models falter. This paper explores how DES can complement this approach by 
enabling simulation-based scenario testing to bridge the data gap.  
This paper aims to persuade the reader that discrete event simulation, hereafter referred to as 
DES5, is an effective tool for predicting AGV movements in a factory setting. By integrating DES 
into traffic management and scheduling, this study addresses the current gap in data-driven 
decision-making for AGV coordination. Through simulation-based analysis, we offer insights 
into optimizing flow efficiency, minimizing congestion, and enhancing scheduling strategies in 
both partially and fully automated environments.

such, this paper has two primary objectives: to provide visibility of factory status and to enable

1.3 Significance of the Study 
Automated machinery and robotics represent large investments for production facilities, yet 
they are often not properly implemented, and their full potential is not harnessed. Automated 
Guided Vehicles (AGVs) have been adopted by over 60% of production facilities,7 primarily for 
material flow. The methods and results in this research paper are transferable knowledge to

companies that wish to understand how to address material flow planning in an automated 
environment.  Moreover, this study also addresses the importance of running simulations of 
factory settings for optimization, measurability, and design. 
Where Machine Learning (ML) and other Data Science predictive methods require large 
amounts of data in order to make accurate predictions, less data-intensive methods, such as 
Discrete Event Simulation (DES), provide a significant advantage in transforming environments 
such as manufacturing. As an example, introducing new machinery to a manufacturing line 
alters the rates and bottlenecks of a system, rendering prior data-driven models unusable, 
whereas DES models can be easily modified to accommodate the new environment. 
With the growing adoption of Digital twins for real-time visualization, scenario testing, and 
data-driven decision-making, understanding the applications of DES in material flow analysis 
has never been more important. This study presents a real example of using simulation to 
optimize traffic flow and predicting congestion points before they occur. Additionally, these 
simulations offer a measurable and reliable method for optimizing flows and understanding 
bottlenecks.

2.1 Process Definition 
Boeing’s 777 models are some of the company’s largest. To make these airplanes more 
fuel-efficient, the newer models, 777X, utilize carbon fiber (composite) wings. Fabrication 
of these wings happens at the Composite Wing Center (CWC), where this project took 
place.

requires the use of a whole aisle, causing potential traffic jams. Consider Figure 1. When a

2.2 Key Concepts 
Understanding the implications and advantages of this paper requires a high-level 
comprehension of the historical background of industrial engineering and widely adopted 
software frameworks. Being DES the primary focus of this research, it’s important to 
understand applications of simulation in factory settings, from Industry 4.0 to the different 
software suites available. Additionally, developing a flexible software product requires 
discussing the paradigms most widely adopted when developing software at scale. 
 
Industry 4.08 –Expanding on the ideas of Industry 3.0, which included robotics, automation, and 
the introduction of computing in the manufacturing world, Industry 4.0, often referred to as the 
fourth industrial revolution, uses big data analytics, machine learning, Internet of Things (IoT),

and cloud computing to improve industrial operations. This study evaluates a novel application 
of data-driven decision-making in Industry 4.0-enabled factories. As market reports predict a 
compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of ~20% from 2024-20329, the applications of DES and 
Digital Twins or factory material flow analysis will likely become increasingly necessary. 
Digital Twin – The term “digital twin” refers to a digital model of a physical object or system10. 
The development of digital twins in manufacturing has gained momentum in recent years, 
particularly with the advent of Industry 4.0. By harnessing the power of advanced sensors and 
cloud computing, creating a digital replica of a physical system is an achievable goal in this era. 
Digital twins, by definition, should mimic the performance of their physical counterparts in real 
time, thus requiring live data updates11. By providing a holistic view of the factory’s state, 
Digital Twins allow their users to visualize potential risk factors and bottlenecks in their 
manufacturing line. It’s crucial to emphasize that the successful implementation of a digital 
twin necessitates an in-depth understanding of the system to be analyzed and accurate up-to-
date measurements of the full state of the system. 
Discrete Event Simulation (DES) – While Digital Twins provide visibility of the current state of a 
system, simulations allow users to conduct experiments, identify risks, and, as proven by this 
work, make predictions of future states of the system. The term “discrete” refers to the 
simulation's behavior in time. The system is evaluated in time jumps defined by a list of 
“events”, and at each time jump, the system’s state is re-computed12. DES is widely adopted as 
the standard simulation technique in the fields of logistics, manufacturing, supply chain, and 
healthcare. Although simple DES systems could be computed by hand, software DES allows 
extremely complex systems to be modeled, and enables the implementation of parallel events 
through the use of an internal event loop.  This internal event loop allows for simulations of 
months or years of data in a few seconds, as well as prallelizing hundreds of events without the

DES Software – In this paper we discuss two main categories of DES software: Software suites 
and open-source libraries. Software suites are usually licensed, have a user interface, and 
simplify many common tasks that users may require. Open-source libraries, on the other hand, 
are mainly targeted towards the programming savvy. The leading software suites include 
AnyLogic, Simio, FlexSim, Arena, SIMUL8, Simulink, and Plant Simulation. Some commonly used 
open-source libraries include SimPy, PowerDEVS, Theta, and DESMO-J used for integrating with 
codebases written in Python, C++, Rust, or Java, respectively. Open-source libraries offer a 
substantial advantage in connectivity and efficiency. Chapter 3 will detail a comprehensive 
exploration of these tools for the problem in this study and the rationale for choosing one

2.3 Key Terminologies, Definitions, and Assumptions 
Producing an effective scheduling solution requires understanding the intricacies of the 
production system. After conducting several interviews with key stakeholders, we compiled the 
following list of terms that are necessary to understand and model the production process and 
design a solution for scheduling.  
There are five main categories worth discussing in this section: Parts, stations, equipment, 
policies, and constraints. We will define the main part types, as well as the particular 
production requirements necessary for producing them. Then, we will discuss some stations 
that handle particular processes worth discussing. It’s important to note that although there 
are over 40 stations in the system, we will only discuss those that significantly contribute to 
traffic management. Next, we discuss the equipment necessary to move parts from one station 
to another. We discuss production and transportation policies currently implemented in the 
system for efficient production. Finally, we talk about general system constraints that introduce 
system limitations. 
Parts 
Alpha Part – One of the three main components produced at the factory, available in four 
distinct variations. For this study, we will refer to them as “Alpha-AX,” “Alpha-AY,” “Alpha-BX,” 
and “Alpha-BY.” Before curing, these parts consume a specific set of Beta components. After 
curing, Alpha parts, which are detached from an Atlas tool at the Liberis Station, require a 
Monorail system for moving. Understanding how these different variations behave is essential 
for accurate simulation. 
Beta Part – One of the three main parts manufactured at the factory. These parts are also 
produced in four variations: “Beta-AX”, “Beta-AY”, “Beta-BX”, and “Beta-BY”. Beta parts go 
through some stations multiple times during a single production run. This implies that the flow 
of Beta parts resembles the following pattern: A->B->C->B->C->… where A, B, and C represent 
stations. This is particularly challenging for traffic planning. Accurately simulating this process 
 
 
24 
 
necessitates tracking which point in the process a part occupies, an insight that cannot be easily 
gained by knowing the station in which the part is located. 
Gamma Part – One of the three main parts manufactured at the factory. With over 70 
variations, the parts are divided into three main categories: Gamma-A, Gamma-B, and Gamma-
C. Each of these categories has a slightly different flow through the factory. Gamma-A is the 
most common type of part and has a specific pre-cure flow. Gamma-B parts follow a different 
pre-cure path, but once they reach the cure stage, they follow the exact same flow as Gamma-A 
parts. Gamma-C parts are quite rare and have been intentionally excluded from this study. 
Gamma parts consume fragments that are used for their initial processing. Understanding all 70 
variations, fragment consumption, and the pathways followed by Gamma parts in detail is 
essential to developing an accurate simulation. 
Stations 
Crucible Station – All three types of parts go through the same Crucible Station. The factory has 
two available Crucible Stations. Prioritizing Crucible utilization is essential. Scheduling of other 
processes may vary once parts arrive at the Crucible on their scheduled time. Additionally, the 
Crucible is situated in a high-traffic area (see Figure 4), so movements in and out of the Crucible 
must be prioritized. As mentioned earlier, performing batch operations at the Crucible is also 
mandatory. When moving four sets of Gamma parts into the Crucible, four AGV moves are 
scheduled. Different policies govern these moves. Utilizing four AGVs for the transports is not 
feasible due to limited availability. Furthermore, to maximize utilization, there is a high 
likelihood that moves out of the Crucible will occur just before new entries. In this scenario, it is 
important to consider using the same type of AGV for both entry and exit. Choosing the right 
policy and implementing it through rule-based algorithms is necessary to compute an effective 
planned move set. 
Nintu Station – Gamma parts attach to an Alpha part at this station. This station has an 
inventory of Gamma “cassettes. " There are 12 different cassettes, with 2 sets of each, totaling 
24 cassettes in the inventory management system. A cassette should be replenished as soon as 
 
 
25 
 
possible. The Gamma inventory and Nintu cassette inventory are regularly evaluated. If a 
cassette can be constructed from the available Gamma parts in inventory, and the Nintu station 
has an available slot for the cassette, it is then constructed and delivered to the Nintu station 
for future use. Moving cassettes into the Nintu station requires the use of AGVs, which affects 
AGV resource usage and contributes to traffic. Therefore, understanding and simulating these 
rules is crucial. Moreover, the Nintu station may be blocked due to a lack of cassette 
availability. 
Equipment 
Transportation Equipment - There are two types of AGVs. AGV-As move Alpha and Gamma 
parts, while AGV-Bs are exclusively for moving Beta parts. After curing, Alpha parts are 
transported using a monorail, Gamma parts are moved with Gamma-carts, and Beta parts are 
conveyed using Beta-carts; therefore, AGVs are no longer utilized. AGVs do not access the 
monorail section; conversely, the monorail cannot reach most areas the AGVs access. These 
physical limitations are crucial when implementing path-planning algorithms for scheduling 
systems. 
Atlas Tools – Pre-cure parts are mounted on Atlas Tools. Each type of Alpha part has two sets of 
Atlas tools. This means that at any given time, there can be up to two of each kind of Alpha 
parts in pre-cure processes. Beta parts follow the same constraint, including two sets for each 
of the four configurations. Gamma parts, on the other hand, include 19 Atlas tools, each with a 
different configuration. These parts occupy physical space and are a limited resource in the 
factory, so scheduling moves requires understanding the available tools and their locations. 
Often, these tools are moved into a storage area to make way for other parts to enter the cell. 
It’s crucial to know where each of these tools is located before initiating the construction of a 
new part. 
Additional Tools—Apart from Atlas tools, Hermes Tools are used to move parts using a crane 
during the post-cure process. Using Hermes tools requires moving the crane to the Hermes 
storage station, performing the required move, and then dropping off the Hermes tool at its 
 
 
26 
 
storage location. A single Hermes-required move involves three moves, each of which will 
affect scheduling and timing.  
Policies 
Path Selection - AGV automation depends on magnets installed on the floor. Due to the size of 
the parts moving through the factory, usually, only one part can move in one lane at any given 
time. This means the lanes are one-way, requiring parts to wait for others to pass before they 
can proceed. If possible, parts should take an alternate route. Some movements on specific 
transporters have constraints, meaning they can only navigate through designated areas. 
Push-Pull Stations- Depending on the desired production rate and the maximum speed of each 
station, stations are classified as either “push” or “pull.” Stations that require nearly maximum 
utility are known as “push” stations. Push stations must create space for incoming parts 
immediately after one part is processed. In other words, parts should move out of push stations 
right away- or as soon as possible- to allow other parts to be produced in that station. In 
contrast, pull stations typically produce at a rate exceeding the required production rate. This 
allows these stations greater flexibility in their utilization. Since pull stations don’t need to 
produce continuously, they aren’t necessarily required to move parts out. Push stations may 
compel parts out of pull stations to prevent pauses in production. Defining which stations are 
pull and which are push is crucial for the development of this study. 
Buffer Station Planning - Intermediate stations, known as buffer stations, are placed 
throughout the factory to ensure that push stations can continue production. These stations 
serve to hold idle parts while waiting for capacity to become available at their next station. Pull 
stations can act as buffer stations if the subsequent station in the sequence is unavailable. If a 
station is full and contains an idle part while a new part attempts to enter, the idle part should 
be moved to an external buffer station to make room for the incoming part. It is crucial to 
incorporate buffer stations in the development of the scheduling algorithm, as they entail 
additional moves within the factory that may not have been initially planned.

complexity to the task at hand. For starters, the entire layup process was restructured in a new 
location within the factory, following completely new logic. This means that, by the time I 
began working there, there was no data related to this new station. The new station comprises 
19 lanes, each linked to a specific set of Gamma-A parts. Each lane can process a particular set 
of 2, 4, or 6 Gamma parts mounted on an Atlas tool; examples of such configurations can be 
seen in Figure 6. Consequently, each Atlas tool is unique to its associated set of Gamma parts 
and lanes. After curing an Atlas tool with a set of Gamma parts, it is free to return to the 
Gamma-A First station and start processing a new set of parts. These capacity constraints and 
part configurations are essential in developing an accurate digital twin of the factory.

AGV idle policy -  Once an AGV set completes a move, one of three things can happen: A new 
team of operators takes over the AGV to perform another move in a nearby station, the AGVs 
are kept in the hallway to be used soon by another station, or the AGVs get driven “home” to 
get charged. Deciding what to do when an AGV goes idle is extremely difficult to do, because 
you have to predict if it’s going to be needed in the near future nearby, but you also have to 
evaluate if the move it’s going to perform is viable with the current AGV battery level. One 
possible policy is just taking the AGV back immediately when a move gets completed, unless

operators currently do. Another possible policy is waiting at the destination for a fixed amount 
of time, to see if any moves come up, and then drive home. The optimal strategy requires the 
system to know exactly what station is going to require that specific set of AGVs next, and move 
them straight there to be used. 
Constraints 
Shifts—Operators work three shifts and avoid making moves near the end of their shift. Certain 
parts of the process require three shifts, while others require only one or two shifts. In general, 
operator availability is a systematic constraint that needs to be considered in this study. 
Batch Operations – Certain operations must wait for others to finish before proceeding. A 
simple example is the processing of Gamma parts in the Crucible station. Before moving the 
Atlas tool into the Crucible, it’s crucial that all Gamma parts remain in a buffer station until a 
complete batch of four Atlas tools is prepared to move into the Crucible station. Similar to 
fragment consumption, batches are a necessary implementation when scheduling moves, as 
they may prevent moves until a completed batch is ready. 
Capacity Limitations - Each cell has a specific capacity. It can hold only a limited number of 
parts at any given time. Before deciding to transfer a part to its next cell, the cell’s capacity 
must be assessed to ensure it can accommodate the incoming part. 
Fragment Consumption - Some stations require multiple parts before they can begin 
processing. The output of these stations is a single part. This occurs in several parts of the 
process, with some stations needing two or three fragments to be combined into a resulting 
part, while the Nintu station combines an average of 12 Gamma parts, represented as 
fragments, into one Alpha part. In this context, a fragment is a physical element that will be 
consumed by another part. Since fragments are essential for processing, their consumption 
logic is crucial to simulate stations as blocked when the necessary fragments are unavailable.

3.1 Overview of the Research Design 
This study follows a simulation-based research approach. It aims to optimize the scheduling of 
moving parts such as AGVs, cranes, and carts and improve traffic flow by simulating material 
movement. The first task for this project involved conducting a series of interviews to 
understand the material flow through the factory, the policies used for selecting and moving 
resources and defining a potential solution. Data was collected to create an accurate real-time 
representation of the factory. DES software was selected after evaluating the available options, 
and finally, the simulation was assembled, validated, and deployed.  
Weekly meetings with the AGV team and the industrial engineering team produced insights 
into this project's requirements and possible implementations. These interviews also 
formulated an understanding of the data structure and future implementations that could 
produce efficiencies. They also allowed us to understand the limitations of the data as well as 
the limitations of the produced simulation with relation to real factory behavior. Business rules 
for decision-making were extracted from subject matter experts (SMEs) at the company. They 
were compiled into a single document and later implemented as rule-based algorithms in a 
structured coding system.  
Deciding what DES system to use consisted of evaluating the possible options and weighing the 
advantages and disadvantages of each. Next, small prototypes were developed on the most 
suitable contenders: SIMIO and SimPy. Finally, SimPy was chosen over SIMIO because it allowed 
for custom logic integration via Python, seamless API connectivity, and better adaptability to 
the company’s existing systems although it requires extensive knowledge of Python 
programming and lacks a user interface. To properly accomplish the goals of this research using 
SimPy, a simple front-end software was developed to generate the visualization of the 
simulation, as well as control certain parameters. This front-end was developed using React.JS, 
as it’s one of the most widely adopted web frameworks. The GUI allows users to visualize real-
time AGV movements, adjust scheduling constraints, and modify resource allocation rules. 
 
 
32 
 
Connecting the SimPy simulation with a front-end requires the development of a simple back-
end API server. Flask was selected as the Python framework for this task. Connecting Flask and 
SimPy together is a trivial process, a clear example of SimPy’s advantage for interconnectivity 
and expandability over SIMIO and other more rigid software.

3.2 Data Collection 
Creating an effective digital twin relies on capturing the factory’s state at any moment.18 Thus, 
identifying reliable sources of information within the company is crucial. This study identifies 
four key data sources in the factory: An automatic data gathering system on certain machines, a 
factory-wide RFID system, an AGV vision system, and an operator stamping system. We explore 
the feasibility of each data source, as well as how the data must be extracted, cleaned, filtered, 
and combined to accurately reflect the factory floor. 
The automatic data gathering system represents a significant advancement toward Industry 
4.0, as it publishes automated data about machine performance and usage. Although this 
system is very powerful, it still requires human input. More importantly, this automated system 
is available on only a subset of machines – approximately 20% of machines in the factory – 
making it unreliable as a source of truth for obtaining a comprehensive understanding of the 
factory’s state.  
The factory's RFID system was configured to track the positions of AGVs at any given time. 
Theoretically, this RFID system can be used alongside any type of RFID tag to detect the 
positions of elements within the factory. This system has great potential and should be further 
explored in the future. These RFID systems are also challenged by potential damage caused by 
the high temperatures experienced at the Crucible station. These systems could provide 
accurate location information, yet progress data would still need to be gathered elsewhere. 
The AGV vision system was briefly explored as a means of detecting which tool was mounted 
on the AGV. This would yield partial information about the part being transported when an AGV 
makes a move. However, this system was never fully configured and would not have served as

an ideal source of information even if it had been. By process of elimination, stamps emerge as 
the only viable source of data, though they are not without their flaws.  
Serving as the primary source of truth for determining both the location and progress of a part 
in the factory flow, stamps are the key data source for this research. Operators manually input 
the stamps into a computerized system. There is a fixed set of stamps along with a planned list 
that must be completed to finalize a part. Stamps are subject to potential human error as 
operators must register them in the system periodically. Alpha parts follow two lists of stamps, 
also known as “orders." During the pre-cure process, an order is followed until reaching the 
Nintu station, which marks a “completed” order; thereafter, a new order is initialized. Delivery 
to final storage concludes the second order for alpha parts. Beta parts adhere to a similar 
structure, with two available orders- one for pre-cure stamps and another for post-cure stamps. 
Lastly, Gamma parts follow six orders: two orders for Gamma-A fragments, two for Gamma-B 
fragments, one for Gamma-A parts, and one for Gamma-B parts. Understanding the logic 
behind how this information is captured is essential for constructing adequate data analysis and 
filtering necessary to replicate the current state of the factory. 
Another drawback of using stamps as a source of truth for part movement is the lack of stamps 
configured for part movement and the absence of a defined stamping process for managing 
buffer stations and Atlas tool locations. In other words, stamps are only approximately reliable 
for confirming the location of a part or tool, and as such, their locations must be inferred using 
a set of rules. Furthermore, the distribution of stamps is not linear. In other words, one stamp 
may represent 50% of the time required to complete a task, while others might affect less than 
1% of the total process time. All of these issues must be considered when building a digital 
model of the factory, as ignoring them would lead to an inaccurate model.

3.3 Tools, Frameworks, and Models 
The development of our solution involved evaluating and selecting the appropriate tool for 
Discrete Event Simulation (DES), constructing an accurate model, and deploying it to its users. 
To achieve these goals, we first conducted a comparative analysis of the DES software services. 
Next, we assembled a comprehensive stack of tools necessary for a viable solution. Then, we

3.4 Solution Design 
The goal of the solution is to create an accurate digital twin of the factory that enables real-
time decision-making, optimizes transport scheduling, and minimizes bottlenecks. This is 
achieved by structuring the system into modular components, each handling a specific aspect 
of factory operations. Once the factory's intricacies are well understood, the data gathered, and 
the problem defined, constructing the solution involves developing a series of functions to run 
and visualize the simulation. By applying Clean Code principles, it’s important to separate data 
entities into well-defined atomic functions and files while utilizing object-oriented programming 
whenever possible. Modeling the material flow with SimPy can be divided into six main 
categories: path planning, transporters, flows, stations, hot-cold start, and utilities. It’s also 
prudent to discuss the design of the API connection and the front-end design.  
3.4.1 Path planning 
It is essential that the simulation has a way of representing the physical space in an accurate 
fashion. To do so, a scale blueprint of the factory floor was used to capture the layout. Figure 
11 shows a representation of the method used. Overlaid on the blueprint, we mapped out a 
series of equidistant points, called “nodes.” These nodes are, therefore, a scaled representation 
of the factory pathways. Since we don’t require keeping track of every node, we extract only 
the nodes that are directly in front of a station. Transporters will inevitably stop at those nodes 
and then enter the station, so it’s not necessary to consider intermediate nodes. Figure 12 
shows, in the final simulation, the resulting nodes that were used. Using a time study, we 
extrapolate a trend line that represents the average time it takes for a transporter to move 
between two nodes. If a shortest path algorithm can be computed, we can then calculate the 
(simulated) time it takes to get from one place to the next. 
Having a mapped set of nodes and the distances between them, we can begin constructing 
networks. Transporters can only navigate within the network they have been assigned. A 
monorail, for instance, which only travels a fixed area of the map, would have a network that 
 
 
42 
 
only includes the nodes in its physical location. This is necessary to avoid issues with path 
planning. When transporters “seize” a node, this node becomes unavailable to other 
transporters. This creates an additional filter, or “mask,” that removes all unavailable nodes 
from the network. The last step of the process is running a shortest path algorithm with the 
nodes available in the network. For calculating the best path between two nodes, we use 
Dijkstra’s algorithm, a well-known algorithm for shortest paths of weighted graphs. Figure 13 
shows how the path planning algorithm may be implemented. The path-planning module also 
ensures real-time congestion management by dynamically updating node availability. If a path 
becomes blocked, transporters can query alternative available routes within their assigned 
network.

3.4.2 Transporters 
The transporter class was designed to represent AGVs, cranes, carts, and the monorail. The 
usage of transporters consists of the following functions: 
1. request_path – Attempts to request a path between the transporter’s current position 
and a destination node. If no path is currently available due to other transporters, this 
function waits for the path to be available. Once a move is requested, the transporter 
seizes all nodes in its path to ensure it reaches its destination. This prevents other 
transporters from entering a “dead-lock” situation. If a node that is in use is requested, 
the requester is added to a queue which follows FIFO logic.  
2. idle_to_home – Once a transporter has reached its destination, this function decides 
what the transporter should do. The options are: Go home, stay on the current node, or 
wait in the current node for some time, if no requests are received, go home.  
3. move – Once a path has been computed, the transporter moves, node by node, to its 
destination. Since the output of the path planning function has already been normalized 
to simulation time, we use these values to move from node to node in the simulation.  
function plan_path(src, dst, mask, network){ 
nodes = apply_mask(mask, network); 
if (src not in nodes or dst not in nodes){ 
 
throw NodesNotInNetwork; 
} 
path = dijkstra_shortest_path(src, dst, nodes); 
return path;  
} 
 
 
44 
 
4. drive_home – Helper function for calculating the path to the predefined “home” node, 
and move the transporter. 
5. go_charge – AGVs require charging once the battery has reached a threshold. Once that 
threshold is reached, the AGVs will be set as inactive and will move home. They stay 
there until fully charged. 
 
3.4.3 Flows 
Alpha, Beta, and Gamma parts move through the factory in particular ways. They use a distinct 
set of transporters, follow a certain order of stations, and take a certain amount of time to 
process at each station. In this paper, we refer to these sets of instructions as the “flow” of 
each part. The step-by-step process that is involved in fetching tools, waiting for material, 
waiting for processing, fetching transporters, loading and offloading, and completing a part is 
defined in these flow files. Every part variation follows its own flow function. And every new 
part in the system initializes a parallel run of a flow function.  
3.4.4 Stations  
Physical locations that can hold parts or process them can be considered either Storage or 
Stations. Stations themselves can serve as storage when processing is complete. This 
differentiation is important because it consists of two distinct capacities in simulation: Storage 
capacity and production capacity. Storage capacity represents how many items can be 
physically located within the station. Alternatively, production capacity indicates how many 
parts can be processed in parallel at a station. When a new part starts processing it consumes 
both of these resources, but once it is completed it no longer uses production capacity, freeing 
up space for other parts to seize that resource and push the current part out of the station if 
necessary. Stations execute the work simulation and publish events when the work is 
completed.  
3.4.5 Hot and Cold Start 
The entry point of any simulation is creating entities which will interact with the simulation. In 
our case, those entities are parts and they can be introduced into the simulation in one of two 
 
 
45 
 
ways: Using real-time data (hot-start) or using a part generator (cold-start). Initial validation 
tests were performed without access to the data, and creating mock data resulted in too 
convoluted work to be worth the effort; therefore, a cold start generator was created. 
Generators can be configured to create parts at any rate and of any type. Testing and 
developing using a cold-start method is ideal since it provides visibility on the complete flow of 
a part. It also enables a controlled flow of parts. Moreover, the cold-start method has several 
advantages in future usage for station processing time analysis and capacity analysis. 
The hot-start method involves the use of either a randomized initial state or a data-fed initial 
state. For either, the part flows must be configured such that a part skips the steps of the flow 
prior to the step of the process the part is at. To illustrate this, imagine a part (P1) whose flow is 
A-B-C-D and has an initial state C(60%), and imagine another part (P2) of the same type and the 
same flow, whose initial state is B(20%). At time zero, P1 shall skip steps A and B, and jump 
directly to C, and complete only the last 40% of the simulated process. In contrast, at the same 
time zero, part B will skip station A and jump directly to station B, completing 80% of the work. 
Schedule prediction depends on the hot-start initialization utilizing the data gathered 
previously. 
3.4.6 Utilities 
Some utility functions implemented in the development of our solution are worth mentioning. 
First, SimPy’s library contains two functions for yielding functions: AnyOf and AllOf. As the 
name suggests, these functions will wait to continue until either any of the specified processes 
completes or all of the specified processes complete. Nonetheless, when gathering resources 
it’s necessary to fetch either resource one or resource two, whichever is available first. Say, for 
instance, a part requires an AGV. The part should send a request to all three AGVs, and select 
the first one that responds. Using SimPy’s “AnyOf” function, all three AGVs are requested as 
expected, but since only one is used and all three received requests, the other two AGVs 
remain blocked indefinitely. This motivated the implementation of a “GetFirstOf” utility, which 
mitigates this issue by releasing and canceling the requests that were not resolved first. 
The second utility function worth mentioning is the push_out function. Recalling the definition 
of push-pull stations in Chapter 2, the push_out function executes the necessary steps to

their buffer locations if available. The algorithm used for this function is best explained by

3.5 Validation and Reliability 
Once the solution has been implemented, validation tests are necessary to assess the system’s 
ability to represent the factory’s state. Validation allows us to determine the reliability of the 
designed solution and, importantly, how it can be adjusted for more accurate results. Reliability 
is measured by comparing the simulated predicted state to the actual future state. Therefore, 
the simulation outcome is limited by the precision of the data collected about the simulation 
state. In this study, we categorize validation into two types: historical data validation and real-
time validation. For our scheduling system to be trustworthy and fully automated, this accuracy 
should approach 100%, with simulated actions occurring within 20 minutes of actual actions. 
Unpredicted disturbances, such as machine breakdowns, should be the only sources of 
inaccuracy. Given the scarcity of data, we do not validate time accuracy, but rather whether a 
move occurs within a 10-hour window. This study refrains from predicting beyond 10 hours into 
the future due to high system volatility.  
It's important to mention that, although the GUI is beneficial for end users, it provides 
significant value in the development process. One of the most powerful features of simulation 
is parallelization. Since events are triggered in parallel, having visibility of the system as a whole 
 
 
51 
 
is essential for debugging logic issues. Visualizing the simulation allows developers to follow 
along with its flow and detect unwanted behaviors and potential causes.  
As mentioned in Chapter 3.3, stamps remain the sole method for determining the state of a 
process. Therefore, refining the simulation with historical data largely depends on stamps. For 
starters, validating the correct usage of buffer stations is not possible using stamps. Moreover, 
the stamps lack timed data indicating when a move actually occurs. Finally, the time window for 
validating historical results is limited. With these considerations in mind, we validated the 
simulation’s results and iterated over new configurations, parameters, and algorithms.  
Validating the efficacy of the simulation against historical results involved loading historical 
data at time t, running the simulation for 10 hours, computing historical data at time t + 10 
hours, and finally constructing a confusion matrix to compare real and predicted moves. The 
10-hour validation window aligns with operational constraints, ensuring reliable short-term 
scheduling. Predictions beyond 10 hours introduce increasing uncertainty due to stochastic 
factory events, requiring manual adjustments or additional constraints for longer-term 
scheduling. Since most production processes take significantly more than 10 hours to complete, 
a small fraction of the active parts in process are moved over a 10-hour window. Thus, relying 
simply on a measurement of accuracy is unreliable. Consider the case where 50 parts are in the 
system and only 3 of them actually move to another station. A model that always predicts zero 
moves would still result in a 94% accuracy. Hence, we rely on TPR as a proxy of simulation 
accuracy as its statistical significance is greater for our use case. Although a single simulation 
run appeared accurate, running the simulation for 30 t values and calculating the average 
accuracy and true positive rate (TPR) revealed issues with the processing times or logic of the 
stations that were subsequently fixed. Figure 19 highlights the significant improvement in 
metrics after adjusting those parameters. The decline in the last iterations was due to 
modifications in the measured timesteps. These additional tests were essential to ensure that 
the fixes did not overfit a single range of data.

they can run specialized simulations that connect directly with our system to obtain usable 
results and metrics to support their claims. This paper clearly demonstrates the necessity and 
advantages of DES for factory automation and optimization. Furthermore, the same system can 
be replicated in any factory where transportation scheduling is a necessity. 
 
4.5 Limitations of the Study 
First, validation of historic results depends on the same data that’s being used to run the 
simulation, so if that data is inaccurate, the simulation results will be inaccurate. Second, many 
stations and flows were simplified for design purposes. The simulation assumes raw material 
 
 
58 
 
supply is always available, but this may not necessarily be the case. Similarly, the last portion of 
the flow consists of inventory storage, an additional rotation, a final inspection, and delivery. 
This additional rotation takes up production space in the rotation station which is used for 
other processes. This could potentially cause a bottleneck that’s not currently modeled in our 
system. The final delivery step also takes over space in the hallway where parts are supposed to 
move through. This poses a potential risk of traffic jams. Conversely, using the simulation can 
help industrial engineers find time windows to perform the necessary moves to complete these 
steps without disturbing production.  
The stamp structure poses a significant challenge for this study. Stamps give us a rough 
estimate of where parts are and how far along in their completion, yet more accurate 
measurements could be gathered by other means, such as cell-specific sensors or RFID sensors 
attached to parts. First, the stamps are operator-dependent, making them subject to human 
error. Second, stamps are only partially accurate as location predictors. Recording parts moving 
into a buffer station would require a sequence-agnostic system for geo-location.  
Chapter 5: Conclusion 
5.1 Key Contributions 
Discrete event simulation has various applications in terms of identifying efficiencies, 
optimization, and predictions in a factory setting. Machine learning models often require large 
amounts of data to be able to accurately predict results. In scenarios where few historical data 
are available, DES is a powerful tool when several interacting parts are present, and 
understanding interactions is important. When using DES as a predictor, stochastic values for 
processing times, movement speed, and machine breakdowns are unnecessary since that 
stochasticity is unpredictable. Instead, we use averages and, on occasion, ideal values to 
understand where and when things potentially could move. Conversely, stochasticity can be 
introduced into the same simulation when analyzing machine rate improvements, throughputs, 
and potential issues with machine breakdowns. A well thought out and developed DES model 
can provide several advantages for industrial planning and execution.  
 
 
59 
 
In parallel, this paper explores the importance of visualization, human-machine interaction, and 
user experience as tools for understanding the state of a manufacturing plant. Developing 
internal tools for visualizing flows and deploying events that connect with external services is a 
complicated task that provides a lot of value. Providing engineers with a clear, holistic 
understanding of the parts moving through the factory automates a significant portion of the 
monotonous activities they perform on a regular basis. 
Finally, the importance of accurate data gathering is fundamental to the growth and success of 
a manufacturing site. Initial data gather setup systems enable a factory to measure accurate 
data as soon as production begins and can even expose potential risks and opportunities in the 
testing phase. It’s not necessary for a factory to have years of historical data to make progress, 
but it is imperative that they can obtain accurate measurements of current data, particularly 
where everything is, how far along in the process parts are, and what general policy is followed 
for moving parts along the factory. If this system is set up correctly, developing predictions 
becomes trivial. 
5.2 Recommendations and Future Work 
Developing this solution presented several opportunities for the company to improve its 
processes, as well as methods for expanding the final solution for other applications. As 
mentioned previously, the most important recommendation is the correct implementation of 
measuring devices to determine the position of parts in the factory. Alternatively, the 
application’s modularity allows for several expansions to be implemented, including AGV policy 
optimization, user experience enhancements, factory section analysis, and capacity analysis. 
The simulation can also be further developed in certain aspects in order to improve accuracy 
and modularity.  
The factory has previously implemented RFID systems that could serve as a viable solution. The 
main challenge with implementing RFID tags attached to parts is the high temperatures 
reached when parts go into the Crucible station, but aside from that, RFID would be an ideal 
solution to measuring where parts are in the factory. Paying attention to accurate data 
gathering, as well as cleaning and maintaining a database system, are fundamental for the 
 
 
60 
 
successful growth of the factory. Systems like the one developed in this paper are highly 
dependent on accurate and clean data, so increasing efforts on this front can potentially result 
in simplifying the development process for other internal tools. 
Having a complete simulated flow of the factory enables the development of an optimal AGV 
selection algorithm. As mentioned in Chapter 3, there are several possibilities for AGV selection 
algorithms and for AGV idle policies. Running the simulation over several randomized 
configurations and measuring certain metrics such as throughput, AGV movement efficiency, 
and part wait time can provide optimal strategies for both AGV selection and idle policy 
selection. In a similar fashion, simulations can be run over a large number of randomized 
scenarios to predict what a modification in capacity or efficiency of a given station can result in. 
These results can be used as leverage by teams to convince management of the estimated 
impact that an investment in a given station can have.  
The final delivered product also has several user experience improvements that can make it 
even more valuable. A feature that was discussed near the end of the development was the 
implementation of a capacity management section in the front-end. What this would look like is 
a UX component that allows users to add capacity and modify production rates to generate a 
new simulation. Some visual enhancements, like having the AGVs move smoothly around the 
screen instead of in time-jumps, would also result in a more seamless user experience. Finally, 
enabling users to close off a section of the factory and implement machine breakdowns can 
allow engineers to use the simulation to represent the factory state more accurately.  
As mentioned at the end of Chapter 4, several simplifying assumptions make the simulation 
slightly inaccurate. Implementing the complete process flow from material sourcing to final 
delivery can be a way in which the application can be further improved. Additionally, 
harnessing the power of the machinery that already contains progress-measuring capabilities 
can be a way to obtain more accurate results when parts enter those stations. This would 
require connecting our system with another internal system not currently available to the 
platform and then combining the data from these two data sources, but it would possibly grant 
more granular details on the state of the factory.
